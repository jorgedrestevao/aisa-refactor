## 1. How to read this file

**Classification** (per `../source-policy.md`): FACT, RECOMMENDATION, CONSTRAINT, TRADE-OFF, RISK, ANTI-PATTERN, DECISION CRITERION, PATTERN.

**Origin tag** on every finding:
- **MS** — Microsoft statement on a page fetched in this pass, with `ms.date` recorded in §14.
- **MS-V** — Microsoft statement on a page authored by the team that sells the recommended alternative (here: the Azure Logic Apps team, I-38). Treated as vendor-side positioning, not neutral guidance: usable for capability facts, **never** for scale or performance adjectives. This matches the rule already fixed in `automation-architecture.md` (its C-03).
- **INF** — analyst inference over documented facts.
- **T3** — independent technical source.
- **UNKNOWN** — not established; the finding says so.

**Absence claims.** Where this file states that a capability does not exist, it says whether a Microsoft page states the absence (MS) or whether it is inferred from the capability not appearing in the reference documentation (INF). INF absence claims are phrased **"treat as unsupported until verified"** everywhere they appear, including in the matrices and boundaries — not only in the finding body. This was a defect corrected in the peer files (their review H-2) and the same discipline applies here.

**Confidence.** HIGH = dated Tier 1 statement fetched in this pass or inherited from a VALIDATED peer. MEDIUM = Tier 1 excerpt-level, aged (> 18 months), inferred, or search-derived. LOW/UNKNOWN = not established; the finding says so.

**Volatility.** Connector throttles, request entitlements, VNet supported-connector lists, preview/GA states and licensing are the fastest-moving facts in this area. Every number is valid on the `ms.date` in §14. Three specific volatility warnings:
1. The Power Platform request model is **explicitly mid-transition** with no announced enforcement date (AT2-03, I-13). A design compliant today can be throttled on a date outside the customer's control.
2. Several connector reference pages carry `ms.date: 2024-03-01` while being *updated* in mid-2026 (I-33, I-34, I-35). The throttle numbers on them are among the most decision-relevant figures in this file and the least well dated. Re-verify before pack encoding.
3. The custom-connector per-connection throttle is **contradicted between two Microsoft pages** (C-01). Do not encode either number without verification.

**Reading rule for verdicts.** STRONG means *no documented constraint is violated* — never "Microsoft endorses". CONDITIONAL means fit depends on a stated, measurable condition. POOR means it collides with a documented limit or an explicit Microsoft "not the best choice". HYBRID means Power Platform for part of the responsibility plus another service beyond its envelope. EXTERNAL means the integration responsibility belongs outside Power Platform entirely. UNKNOWN means not established in this corpus — do not infer.

**Overall confidence MEDIUM-HIGH.** Raised above MEDIUM because the load-bearing constraints (gateway payload caps, VNet scope and exclusions, connector throttles, flow timeout/retry/concurrency envelope, batch semantics, webhook and Service Bus contracts, managed-identity scope) are all fetched and dated in this pass, and because Microsoft's own Architecture Center now publishes an explicit integration-requirements framework (I-01) that can be used as the spine rather than inferred. Capped below HIGH because: (1) **no empirical throughput or latency benchmark exists anywhere in this corpus** for any connector, gateway node, or broker — every performance statement is limit-based, not measurement-based (U-04); (2) two Microsoft pages disagree on the single most-cited connector limit (C-01) and one Microsoft page contradicts itself on SAP authorization (C-03); (3) research, review and remediation share one author and one session — independent spot-checking is recommended, and the four highest-value checks are IA-12 (the 100 calls/60 s HTTP-with-Entra-ID throttle, because it silently caps the most common "just call the API" design), IA-22 (concurrency control can *drop* triggers), IA-31 (managed identity covers plug-ins only), and IA-45 (the SAP authorization contradiction).

## 2. The integration decision spine

Microsoft's Architecture Center now states the starting point explicitly: *"Successful integration design starts with understanding three foundational dimensions: volume and frequency, directionality, and capability"* (I-01, `ms.date` 2025-12-11). Those three are necessary but not sufficient — the Block A brief lists twelve requirement dimensions, and the fetched evidence supports all twelve. §2.1 runs each as **REQUIREMENT → CONSTRAINT → ARCHITECTURAL CONSEQUENCE**; §2.2 gives the ordered classification test that turns the answers into a topology.

### 2.1 Twelve requirement dimensions

### 2.2 The ordered classification test

Apply in order; the first "yes" fixes the integration topology class. This is the routing device the pack will need; the classes themselves are defined as patterns in `architecture-patterns.md`.

1. **Is the integration responsibility already owned elsewhere in the enterprise?** (An existing ESB, iPaaS, API gateway, message backbone, or a system-owning team with a published contract.) → **EXTERNAL**. Power Platform consumes the published contract and integrates nothing. See §12 — this is the question the pack must ask *first*, and the one most often skipped.
2. **Must a message not be lost, duplicated or reordered — or must it survive the consumer being down?** → **EVENT-BROKERED / QUEUE-BASED**. The transport carries the guarantee (I-12); Power Platform is a producer or an idempotent consumer, never the guarantee itself.
3. **Do three or more Power Platform artefacts, or two or more consumer classes, need the same backend capability — or must the backend contract be versioned, rate-limited, transformed or observed independently of its consumers?** → **API-MEDIATED** (§4.2, AP-02).
4. **Does the work exceed a synchronous window (120 s in/out, 180 s canvas, 2 min plug-in) or need to survive restarts?** → **ASYNCHRONOUS / BACKGROUND** (AP-09), often composed with 2.
5. **Is the requirement to *read* reference data owned elsewhere, with acceptable source freshness and no need for platform data features?** → **DATA VIRTUALIZATION** (AP-06). If platform data features (audit, row-level security, offline, search, charts) are required on that data → **REPLICATION** (AP-07), and the synchronization risk register in §8 applies in full.
6. **Otherwise: is this one Power Platform artefact calling one system endpoint, at low volume, with a stable contract and a single consumer?** → **DIRECT** (§3, AP-01). This is the correct answer far more often than architects trained on enterprise integration expect — Microsoft's own guidance is *"prioritize solutions that meet business needs with minimal complexity… Choose the simplest approach that fulfills requirements and avoids unnecessary investment"* (I-01) and *"start with Power Automate as the default option"* (I-02).

**Why the order matters.** Running the test in this order prevents the two symmetrical failure modes the brief names: adding an API layer or a broker to a two-endpoint problem (over-engineering, §6 X-11), and hand-rolling reliability inside a flow for a requirement that already has an enterprise owner (under-engineering, §6 X-01 / X-09). Step 1 before step 6 is what stops "we built our own integration layer in Power Automate" from becoming the default outcome.

- **Classification:** DECISION CRITERION. **Origin:** INF (the ordering is this file's synthesis; every constraint behind it is MS-sourced in §5). **Confidence:** MEDIUM. **Sources:** synthesis over IA-01…IA-49; I-01, I-02.

## 4. Decision boundaries — DIRECT / API-MEDIATED / EVENT-BROKERED / EXTERNAL

Each boundary is stated as the condition that *crosses* it. Below the condition the simpler topology is not merely acceptable but preferred (I-01, I-02). Above it, the simpler topology violates a documented constraint or a stated requirement.

### 4.1 DIRECT → stays DIRECT while all of the following hold

- Consumers of the backend capability number **one** (one app, or one flow). Two or more consumers of the same capability is the trigger for boundary 4.2 (INF over I-31: an API layer *"centralizing the logic where it could be used by other applications in the organization"*).
- Projected calls stay inside the connector throttle for the mechanism chosen in §3, measured **per connection**, at the projected *frequency* peak, not the average (I-01, I-13).
- The backend contract is stable, or the customer accepts the republish-plus-reconnect cost on every change (I-07).
- The call completes inside 120 s outbound / 180 s from canvas (I-13, PS-12).
- No transformation or business rule needs to exist in more than one place.
- Failure semantics of "retry per the flow's default policy, then fail the run and log it" are acceptable — noting that the default policy itself depends on the owner's licence profile (IA-17).
- The backend's own authorization model is sufficient, and per-user authorization at the backend is either not required or achievable with the connector's explicit-identity option (DA-33).

### 4.2 DIRECT → API-MEDIATED when any of the following becomes true

- **Reuse:** ≥ 2 consumer classes (apps, flows, agents, external systems) need the same backend capability. Microsoft's own framing: *"shifting the logic from the canvas app to a RESTful API… centralizing the logic where it could be used by other applications in the organization"* (I-31, MS).
- **Chattiness / composition:** the client needs data from several backends per screen or per step. *"when multiple data sources are needed to build a single view, using an API layer can help to deliver a performant experience, because the data response can be shaped server-side and delivered to the client more efficiently"* (I-31, MS); *"evaluation of how Power Apps using different data sources directly might be too chatty with the data sources"* (I-31, MS). The Azure pattern that names this is Gateway Aggregation (I-46, AP-08).
- **Contract and versioning:** the backend must evolve without breaking consumers. APIM's gateway *"acts as a facade to the backend services, allowing API providers to abstract API implementations and evolve backend architecture without impacting API consumers"* (I-20, MS).
- **Rate limiting / protection of the backend:** the requirement is to protect the backend from the consumers. APIM *"Enforces usage quotas and rate limits"* (I-20, MS). Note that this is the *only* documented place in the fetched evidence where **Power Platform-side** callers can be rate-limited by policy — Power Automate itself has no back-pressure construct beyond concurrency control, which has its own defect (IA-22).
- **Observability:** the requirement is end-to-end tracing across a client operation that fans out to several backends. *"collect telemetry across the full request path… This visibility is important in a gateway aggregation pattern because a single client operation depends on multiple back-end calls"* (I-46, MS).
- **Caching:** the backend cannot meet the frequency requirement and a server-side cache is the remedy. *"The use of this middle-tier layer means that you can add a server-side caching layer and implement richer telemetry for the app"* (I-31, MS); APIM *"If configured, caches responses to improve response latency and minimize the load on backend services"* (I-20, MS).
- **Semantic translation from a legacy contract:** the backend's data model would otherwise leak into Dataverse and into every flow. This is the Anti-Corruption Layer condition (I-47, AP-08 alternative): *"When you maintain access between new and legacy systems, you force the new system to adhere to at least some of the legacy system's APIs or other semantics… this support corrupts what might otherwise be a cleanly designed modern application"* (I-47, MS).
- **Private-network reach with credential isolation:** the reference architecture for this is explicit — custom connector + Entra-ID-protected backend + subnet-delegated environment + client id/secret pulled from Key Vault through environment variables (I-27, MS).
- **Consumption governance / discovery:** the API should be discoverable and subscribable rather than reachable. APIM's developer portal plus the one-click *"export APIs from API Management to the Power Platform… as custom connectors for discovery and consumption by citizen developers"* (I-23, MS) is the sanctioned mechanism.

**When API-MEDIATED is unnecessary complexity** (the brief asks for this explicitly):
- One consumer, one backend, stable contract, inside the throttle → the API layer adds a hop, a bill, a team and a deployment unit with no requirement behind it. I-01's closing instruction applies: *"Balance technical capability with cost, licensing, and maintenance requirements. Choose the simplest approach that fulfills requirements and avoids unnecessary investment"* (MS).
- The backend is already an enterprise-managed API. Wrapping a managed API in a second managed API duplicates contract management; the correct action is to consume it (possibly via a custom connector generated from its OpenAPI definition).
- The only motivation is "best practice" or a wish to look like a reference architecture. The FINAL TEST for this area is deliberately *"Which architectural structure best satisfies these requirements?"* — not *"Which Microsoft reference architecture looks similar?"*
- The API layer would be a pass-through with no policy, no transformation, no caching and no aggregation. A gateway that only forwards is a single point of failure with no compensating benefit: *"The gateway service might introduce a single point of failure (SPoF)"*, *"The gateway might introduce a bottleneck"* (I-46, MS).
- **Beware the aggregation-in-the-gateway trap.** Even when a gateway is justified, heavy logic does not belong in it: *"Rather than build aggregation into the gateway, consider placing an aggregation service behind the gateway"* and *"If the aggregation requires custom domain logic, complex transformations, or longer-running orchestration, place that functionality in a dedicated custom service behind the gateway"* (I-46, MS).

### 4.3 → EVENT-BROKERED / QUEUE-BASED when any of the following becomes true

- **The message must not be lost when the consumer is down.** *"tasks aren't delayed if the service isn't available when they post messages to the queue"* (I-44, MS).
- **Spiky arrival that would otherwise throttle the target.** Microsoft's own worked Power Platform example escalates in exactly this order: direct → decouple reads through a data lake → *"To decrease the dependency even further, decouple the create and update requests from the website using a queuing service such as Azure Service Bus"*, with the stated purpose *"prevent throttling errors (such as HTTP 429 Too Many Requests)"* (I-01, MS).
- **Ordering, duplicate detection, dead-lettering or transactional messaging is required.** Only Service Bus offers all four of the properties in I-12's table; Event Hubs has **no** dead-lettering; Event Grid has no ordering, no transactions, no duplicate detection (I-12, MS).
- **Scheduled future delivery with cancellation.** Microsoft's notification reference architecture schedules Service Bus messages and keeps *"sequence numbers for future updates or cancellations"*, explicitly *"without the overhead that Power Automate-based delays would introduce"* (I-29, MS).
- **The producer and consumer must scale independently.** *"It helps maximize scalability because the number of queues and the number of services can vary to meet demand"* (I-44, MS).

**What the broker does not fix, and must be designed for:**
- **At-least-once means duplicates.** *"Most queue services deliver messages with at-least-once semantics, which means that consumers can receive the same message more than once. Design consumer logic to be idempotent"* (I-44, MS). In Dataverse the idempotency key mechanism is alternate keys, with their own limits (DA-53).
- **A queue is not infinite.** *"If your average producer rate exceeds the consumer rate, the queue continues to grow and latency increases. Monitor queue depth and scale consumers within safe limits, or shed work at the producer"* (I-44, MS).
- **Autoscaling the consumer can just move the overload.** *"Autoscaling without bounding consumers' aggregate downstream rate only moves the overload to downstream dependencies"* (I-44, MS) — decisive when the consumer writes back into Dataverse, whose service protection limit is per identity (DA-10).
- **Ordering is not preserved by default.** *"Introducing a queue between a producer and consumer doesn't preserve the original submission order under all conditions, especially when multiple consumers process messages in parallel"* (I-44, MS).
- **Poison messages need a destination.** *"route them to a dead-letter queue… Monitor dead-letter queue depth"* (I-44, MS). **Power Automate has no documented dead-letter construct of its own — treat as unsupported until verified** (INF over I-13's error-handling surface, which lists retry policy, run-after and scopes only; U-11).
- **The broker path currently forfeits private networking from Dataverse.** *"Azure-aware plugins don't support VNet"* (I-10, MS) — so "events out of Dataverse to Service Bus" and "all Power Platform egress inside my VNet" cannot both be satisfied through the Azure-aware plug-in mechanism today (IA-28).

### 4.4 → EXTERNAL (Power Platform is not the integration layer) when any of the following is true

See §12 for the full treatment with evidence. In summary form, the boundary is crossed when: the enterprise already owns the integration capability and a contract exists; the requirement includes B2B/EDI protocol handling; strict cross-system transactionality or saga compensation is required; sustained throughput exceeds the three-meter envelope with no natural partitioning; the integration must run inside a network the platform cannot enter; retention/replay of message history beyond 30 days is required; the integration must be deployed and versioned as code with infrastructure-as-code and zero-downtime slots; or the integration must survive the departure of its author without a licence and ownership migration exercise.

- **Classification:** DECISION CRITERION. **Origin:** MS for every quoted condition; INF for the boundary framing and the ordering. **Confidence:** HIGH for the quoted conditions; MEDIUM for the framing. **Sources:** I-01, I-02, I-12, I-20, I-23, I-27, I-29, I-31, I-44, I-46, I-47; peers DA-10, DA-33, DA-53, PS-12.

## 5. Findings

### 5.1 Requirement dimensions — the framework and its gaps

#### IA-01 — Microsoft's own integration framework starts from three dimensions, and its worked example is a **capacity-planning** exercise over the investment horizon, not a technology comparison
- **Classification:** RECOMMENDATION + DECISION CRITERION · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"The first step is to deconstruct the requirements into the three key components of an integration: Volume and frequency… Directionality… Capability"*. The worked example computes `[Customers] × [Cases per customer] × [Average updates per case]`, then: *"if the system handles 10 million updates per year today and grows at 20% annually, the integration must support 25 million updates per year over five years"*, and *"To ensure the integration remains effective over a typical five-year return-on-investment (ROI) period, design the solution to support at least 25 million requests per year"* (I-01, `ms.date` 2025-12-11).
- **Why it matters:** It reframes integration design as a sizing decision with a horizon, which is what makes a mechanism choice defensible. It also gives the pack a numeric question that a business stakeholder can actually answer.
- **Decision impact:** Any integration requirement should be captured as a *projected* volume at a *stated* horizon with a *stated* growth rate, plus a frequency distribution. Without those three numbers the mechanism choice in §3 cannot be made and must be recorded as Unknown, not assumed.
- **Conditions:** The page's own framing is Power-Automate-centric (*"This section is intended primarily for enterprise architects involved in designing new or optimizing existing system integrations using Power Automate low-code solutions"*, I-03). Its dimensions generalise; its conclusions lean toward cloud flows and must be read with that bias in view.
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-01, I-03

#### IA-02 — Volume and frequency are separate requirements and can legitimately produce **two** integrations for one dataset
- **Classification:** DECISION CRITERION · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"Two integration scenarios might involve the same total volume, such as 60,000 records per hour and 1,000 records per minute, but differ in frequency. While both equal the same hourly volume, the minute-by-minute expectation changes the solution design. - Don't assume one solution fits both. - Validate the system's ability to handle higher-frequency load. - Consider building separate solutions if one pattern is resource-intensive or rarely used"* (I-01).
- **Why it matters:** The reflex is one pipeline per data flow. Microsoft's guidance sanctions splitting a single logical flow into a high-frequency thin path and a low-frequency bulk path — which is exactly the shape of the sync reference architecture (I-28: event-driven flows for record-level updates plus nightly dataflows for bulk and reconciliation).
- **Decision impact:** For any bidirectional or high-volume requirement, evaluate a **split design** (event path + batch path) before evaluating a single mechanism. The split also gives reconciliation for free (IA-40).
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-01, I-28

#### IA-03 — Trigger class is a requirement, not an implementation detail: scheduled is predictable in time and unpredictable in volume; event-driven is the reverse
- **Classification:** TRADE-OFF · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** Scheduled: *"Run at fixed intervals. Easier to predict and manage. Suitable for stable data growth patterns."* Event-driven: *"Launch based on user actions or system events. Harder to predict. Can spike unexpectedly, especially in public-facing systems."* (I-01). And on schedules specifically: *"Scheduled triggers offer flexible recurrence options, from once per second to once per year. They're predictable in timing but can become unpredictable in volume if data scope expands or growth exceeds expectations"*, with the failure mode named: *"a flow designed to run every 10 minutes might fail if it starts taking longer than 10 minutes to complete"* (I-02).
- **Why it matters:** Each trigger class has a *different* dominant risk. Scheduled work fails by overlapping itself; event work fails by spiking. The mitigation for each is different (run-duration monitoring and alerting vs. buffering and rate limiting), so the choice determines the operational design.
- **Decision impact:** Record the trigger class per stream, and attach the matching operational control: for scheduled, a runtime-versus-interval alert (*"Monitor flow execution time to avoid overlaps or delays"*, I-02); for event-driven, a spike plan (*"Implement throttling or rate-limiting mechanisms. Prepare a mitigation plan for unexpected spikes in event frequency"*, I-02).
- **Conditions:** Seasonality is called out separately as its own planning input: *"Monthly or quarterly billing cycles might cause predictable surges. Tax season or public service deadlines can create unpredictable spikes"* (I-01).
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-01, I-02

#### IA-04 — Directionality is decided by the network posture of the constrained party, and the documented remedy is to **invert the caller**
- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT + PATTERN · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"High-security environments often impose strict access controls that influence integration architecture. Legacy on-premises systems might restrict inbound connections. In such cases, design the integration so the legacy system initiates communication with the cloud application"* (I-01). Symmetrically, the gateway exists precisely to avoid the inverse requirement: *"It provides quick and secure data transfer and requires no inbound ports to your network—only outbound ports to reach the Azure web service to which the gateway connects"* (I-09).
- **Why it matters:** Most integration debates that present as "which connector" are actually "who is allowed to open the connection". The answer is a network-security fact, not a platform preference, and it changes the topology entirely: an inverted caller means Power Platform must expose an inbound endpoint (HTTP-triggered flow, Dataverse custom API, or Web API with an application user) and the whole auth and throttling analysis moves to the inbound side.
- **Decision impact:** Ask the network owner, per stream, *who may initiate*. Three answers, three topologies: Power Platform initiates → connector/gateway/VNet; the enterprise system initiates → inbound endpoint plus application-user identity (I-24's external-events pattern is the sanctioned shape); neither may initiate directly → a broker in the middle that both sides reach outbound.
- **Conditions:** Requires involving *"infrastructure architects and security officers"* (I-01) — the pack should treat these as named stakeholders for any integration requirement, not optional reviewers.
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-01, I-09, I-24

#### IA-05 — One business goal decomposes into N *streams*, and each stream is an independent design decision
- **Classification:** DECISION CRITERION · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** The worked example takes a single requirement ("keep case information in sync between an external customer and internal service engineers") and decomposes it into four streams: *"A data stream from the website to write case updates into Dataverse; Another stream for the website to read updates from Dataverse; A third data stream where engineers write updates into Dataverse from the Power App; A final data stream to read updates in the Power App"* (I-01). The four streams then get **different** treatments in the same article: reads are offloaded to a data lake, writes are decoupled through a queue (I-01).
- **Why it matters:** This is the single most useful structural idea in the fetched Microsoft material, and it is the one most often skipped. Counting streams rather than system pairs is what surfaces the read/write asymmetry that drives the fully-decoupled design.
- **Decision impact:** The pack must elicit integrations as a **stream inventory** (source, target, direction, volume, frequency, latency class, consistency class), not as a list of "integrations with SAP". Per-stream verdicts are legitimate and expected; a single verdict for a system pair is a smell.
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-01

#### IA-06 — "Weakest link" is Microsoft's stated capability rule, and it makes the *other* system's limits part of the Power Platform decision
- **Classification:** DECISION CRITERION · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"Integration performance depends on the capability of each system involved. The weakest system in the chain limits the overall result. - Evaluate system capabilities against business requirements. - Identify bottlenecks that might affect high-frequency or large-volume data transfers. - Consider enhancements if a system can't meet performance expectations."* And: *"A system that performs well once a day might fail under multiple daily loads. - Match system capability to the required frequency. - Don't assume that volume alone determines feasibility"* (I-01).
- **Why it matters:** It defeats the common corpus-level bias of researching only the Power Platform side. If SAP, the ERP, or the legacy midrange system cannot take 1,000 calls/minute, no connector choice matters. The consequence — that the enterprise system's own throughput must be captured as a requirement input — is what makes the analysis honest.
- **Decision impact:** For every stream, capture the **target system's** documented or measured capability, and record it as Unknown when the owning team cannot state it. An Unknown here is a decision-blocking Unknown for high-frequency streams: proceeding is a bet on someone else's capacity.
- **Conditions:** The evidence says to engage *"System administrators"* and *"centralized or decentralized IT teams to validate assumptions"* (I-01).
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-01

#### IA-07 — When the weak end cannot be improved, Microsoft's named remedies are **caching/replication** and **buffering** — both of which trade a different requirement away
- **Classification:** TRADE-OFF · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"Caching is a common solution when a system can't meet performance requirements. - Use tools like Azure Synapse Link for Dataverse to replicate data into scalable storage. - Understand the trade-off: caching improves response time but might deliver outdated data. - Ensure data remains fresh to prevent inaccurate results in real-time processes"* (I-01). Buffering: the queue *"acts as a buffer and stores the message until the service retrieves it"* and *"It helps control costs because you only need enough service instances to meet the requirements for an average load rather than the peak load"* (I-44).
- **Why it matters:** There are only two structural moves once the weakest link is fixed, and each spends a different currency: caching spends **freshness**, buffering spends **latency**. Naming the currency makes the trade explicit for the business rather than hidden in a technical choice.
- **Decision impact:** Requirement "the weak system cannot meet the frequency" → ask which of freshness or latency the business will sell. If neither, the requirement itself has to change or the weak system has to be re-platformed; there is no third mechanism in the fetched evidence.
- **Conditions:** Replication then imports the full synchronization risk register (§8), including the freshness floors in DA-49 and the reconciliation obligation in IA-40.
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-01, I-44; peer DA-49

### 5.2 Latency, synchrony and the shape of a call

#### IA-08 — "Instant" is not instant, and Microsoft says so; the user-facing consequence must be designed, not assumed
- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT + RISK · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"Instant triggers aren't truly instant. Execution time depends on system availability and transformation complexity. - Add visual indicators in the app to show progress and allow cancellation if the operation takes too long. - In large organizations, simultaneous requests from many users can strain the system. - Integrations can fail for various reasons. Ensure the app provides feedback to users during execution. Avoid scenarios where users select a button and receive no response, which leads to a poor user experience"* (I-02).
- **Why it matters:** The user-driven integration is the most common shape in low-code work and it couples the *user's* experience directly to the *enterprise system's* latency and availability. Every synchronous button-press integration is an availability dependency inherited by the UI.
- **Decision impact:** Any user-triggered integration must specify: the timeout budget, the progress affordance, the cancel affordance, and the failure message. Concurrency at peak must be estimated (*"simultaneous requests from many users"*) — a per-user integration multiplied by the user count is a throughput requirement in disguise.
- **Cross-reference:** the app-side handling belongs to `application-architecture.md`; the platform's synchronous ceilings are PS-12 (canvas 180 s) and PS-28 (flow 120 s in/out).
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-02; peers PS-12, PS-28

#### IA-09 — The synchronous window is 120 s in and 120 s out, but the **asynchronous** outbound window is configurable up to 30 days — this is the sanctioned escape, and it changes the contract
- **Classification:** FACT + PATTERN · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"Outbound synchronous request | 120 seconds (2 minutes) | Examples of outbound requests include calls made by HTTP triggers. **Tip**: For longer-running operations, use an asynchronous polling pattern or an 'Until' loop."* *"Outbound asynchronous request | Configurable up to 30 days."* *"Inbound request | 120 seconds (2 minutes)… Flows that contain a response action including Respond to Copilot, HTTP Response, and Respond to a PowerApp or flow always return a response within this limit. Child flows that start before the response action continue running separately, and actions after the response action continue running beyond this limit, enabling a flow to respond and continue running other operations"* (I-13, `ms.date` 2026-07-17).
- **Why it matters:** Three decision-relevant facts in one place. (1) The 120 s ceiling is not extendable for synchronous calls. (2) The platform *does* support long-running outbound work — up to 30 days — but only through the asynchronous pattern, which requires the **backend** to implement HTTP 202 + `Location` + polling (I-45). (3) "Respond then continue" is a documented, supported shape: a flow can answer the caller inside 120 s and keep working afterwards. That is the Power Automate expression of asynchronous request-reply.
- **Decision impact:** Requirement "the operation takes 4 minutes" → do not try to make the call synchronous. Either (a) respond-then-continue with a status record the client polls, or (b) require the backend to implement the 202/`Location`/`Retry-After` contract, or (c) move the work behind a queue and a worker. Option (b) is a **requirement on the other team**, and must be raised with them, not assumed.
- **Conditions:** The HTTP-with-Entra-ID connector explicitly does **not** support this shape: *"The asynchronous pattern based on the response Location header is not supported"* (I-35). So mechanism choice and long-running capability interact.
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-13, I-35, I-45

### 5.3 Volume, throughput, throttling and back-pressure

#### IA-10 — Throughput must be expressed in three units because three independent meters are evaluated separately, and Microsoft warns that the connector meter usually bites first
- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT + DECISION CRITERION · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** Entitlement: *"Power platform requests per 5 minutes | 100,000"*, *"Power platform requests per 24 hours | 10,000 for Low; 200,000 for Medium; 500,000 for High; 10,000,000 for Unlimited Extended"* (I-13). Service protection: 6,000 requests / 1,200 s execution / 52 concurrent per user per 5-minute sliding window per web server (PS-18, DA-10, AT2-04). Connector: *"Individual connectors have their own limits, which you often reach before the limits mentioned previously. Be sure to check the documentation for your connector"* (I-13), and separately *"API request limits are different from connector throttling limits. They apply to all runs across all your flows in a 24-hour period"* (I-22).
- **Why it matters:** This is the corpus's most reused fact (AT2-02) and it is restated here because in an *integration* context the third meter is the one that decides the topology: SharePoint at 600/60 s (I-34), Dataverse at 6,000/300 s (I-33) and HTTP-with-Entra-ID at **100/60 s** (I-35) are three completely different architectures for the same nominal volume.
- **Decision impact:** Before choosing a mechanism, compute: actions/day (entitlement), requests per 5 min per identity (service protection), calls per connection per window (connector). Pick the smallest. If the requirement exceeds it, the options are partition across connections/identities, batch, or move the path off connectors entirely — and only the third is a genuine architecture change.
- **Conditions:** *"Both successful and failed actions count toward limits, but not skipped actions"*; *"Retries and extra requests from pagination count as actions"*; *"A simple flow with one trigger and one action results in two actions each time the flow runs"* (I-22). Retry storms therefore consume the very budget they are trying to recover.
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-13, I-22, I-33, I-34, I-35; peers PS-18, DA-10, AT2-02, AT2-04

#### IA-11 — Connector throttles are **per connection**, which makes the connection a capacity-planning object shared across artefacts
- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT + RISK · **Origin:** MS (the per-connection wording) + T3 (the shared-quota consequence)
- **Evidence:** The reference pages state the unit explicitly: SharePoint *"API calls per connection | 600 | 60 seconds"* (I-34); Dataverse *"API calls per connection | 6000 | 300 seconds"* (I-33); HTTP with Microsoft Entra ID *"API calls per connection | 100 | 60 seconds"* (I-35). T3 corroboration of the consequence: *"The count is per connection, not per flow, so multiple flows using the same connection share this quota"* (I-41, 2026-04-14).
- **Why it matters:** Two well-behaved flows can throttle each other without either exceeding its own budget, because quota is attached to the connection object rather than the artefact. This is invisible in design and in single-flow testing, and it is the mechanism behind most "it worked in test" throttling incidents.
- **Decision impact:** Treat connections as capacity units: enumerate which artefacts share each connection, and where a stream needs guaranteed headroom, give it a dedicated connection (and, where the identity matters, a dedicated service principal — *"Distribute loads across service principles rather than a single user"*, DA-10/AT2-04). Record the connection topology as part of the integration design.
- **Conditions:** The per-connection framing is MS-sourced from the reference pages' own table headers; the *sharing* consequence is stated by a T3 source and is a straightforward reading of the unit rather than an independent claim. **Confidence:** HIGH on the unit; MEDIUM on the shared-quota consequence pending a Tier 1 statement (U-15).
- **Sources:** I-33, I-34, I-35, I-41; peers DA-10, AT2-04

#### IA-12 — The "HTTP with Microsoft Entra ID (preauthorized)" connector — the usual answer to "just call the API with the user's identity" — is throttled at **100 calls per connection per 60 seconds** and cannot carry binary payloads
- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT + RISK · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"API calls per connection | 100 | 60 seconds"*. Plus: *"The connector encodes the request body into base64 encoding, hence it should be used to call backend services which expect the request body in this format. You cannot use this connector to call a backend service that expects the request body in raw binary format."* *"Requests that include binary content (for example, PDF files, images, or Office documents) are not supported and may result in corrupted or unreadable files. This behavior is by design; only text-based payloads are supported."* *"The connector is based on multi-tenant application registration… Therefore, we only support specifying resources under users' default tenant ('common')."* *"Resources based on ADFS SSO… are not supported."* *"The asynchronous pattern based on the response Location header is not supported."* *"The use of cookie authorization request header is not supported in case of on-premises data gateway enabled connection."* *"Removing or adding preauthorizations can take up to 1 hour to reflect for connections existing prior to the update."* (I-35).
- **Why it matters:** This connector is the low-friction path to delegated-identity API calls and it is also VNet-supported (I-10), which makes it attractive for private-network designs. Its throttle is **six times tighter than SharePoint's and sixty times tighter than Dataverse's**, and its payload restrictions rule out the document-handling scenarios low-code solutions most often need. Choosing it for convenience and discovering the ceiling in production is a predictable failure.
- **Decision impact:** Use it for low-volume, text-payload, delegated-identity calls to Entra-protected endpoints in the user's home tenant. For anything else — volume, binaries, cross-tenant, ADFS, long-running — the documented fallback is stated on the page itself: *"If your scenario requires something more advanced, please use the 'HTTP' connector or create a custom connector"* (I-35). Note that the fallback changes the identity model (custom connector cannot use `client_credentials`, I-08) and the governance posture.
- **Conditions:** Scope grants require a Global Administrator to create `oauth2PermissionGrants` via a Microsoft-published PowerShell script; a partially granted scope set yields a **runtime** 403 rather than a connection-time failure: *"If a scope (permission) has been granted but not all of the required scopes are included, the creation of the connection will succeed but you will encounter a Forbidden (403) error at runtime"* (I-35). That is a deployment-time trap: the connection tests green and fails in use.
- **Volatility:** The page's `ms.date` is 2024-03-01 (updated 2026-06-24) and it announces a *new version of the connector without preauthorization* to allow discrete consent. Whether the throttle and restrictions carry over is **not stated** (U-07).
- **Confidence:** HIGH on the quoted restrictions; MEDIUM on their currency given the `ms.date` · **Sources:** I-35, I-08, I-10

#### IA-13 — Custom connectors are capped in **count** as well as in rate, and the count cap is licence-dependent
- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"Number of custom connectors | 50 per user"* (I-13). Per I-08: *"Number of custom connectors that you can create | Free plan: one; Office 365 and Dynamics 365 plans: one; Per user plan: 50"*, with the footnote *"The free plan doesn't allow you to share connections or custom connectors with other people in your company. Dynamics scaling of connectors isn't supported in a pay-as-you-go environment, and so the number of custom connectors that can be created is one."* Logic Apps by contrast: *"1,000 per Azure subscription"* (I-08). Definition-file cap: *"If you use an OpenAPI definition or Postman collection to describe a custom connector, the file must be less than 1 MB"* (I-08). Logic Apps Swagger structural caps: 256 operations, 512 schemas per body, 16,384 schemas per operation (I-08).
- **Why it matters:** An API-per-capability strategy implemented as one custom connector per API hits a per-user ceiling, and on Microsoft 365 / Dynamics 365 seeded rights the ceiling is **one**. This is a licensing-driven architecture constraint of exactly the kind PS-43 warns about, and it pushes designs toward **one connector fronting many operations** — which in turn is an argument for a real API gateway (§4.2) rather than a connector sprawl.
- **Decision impact:** Requirement "many backend capabilities" → prefer a small number of coarse-grained connectors over many fine-grained ones, and check the makers' licences before assuming 50. If the answer is one connector for the whole estate, an APIM-fronted API with multiple operations is the natural shape (I-20, I-23).
- **Conditions:** The apparent conflict between "50 per user" (I-13) and the tiered list (I-08) resolves as scope, not contradiction: I-13 states the per-user-plan case. Registered as C-02 (resolved).
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-13, I-08; peer PS-43

#### IA-14 — Content **throughput** (bytes through run history) is a separately metered ceiling that document-heavy integrations hit before the request meters
- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"Content throughput per 5 minutes | 120 MB for Low; 1.2 GB for all others"*; *"Content throughput per 24 hours | 200 MB for Low; 2 GB for Medium; 10 GB for High"* (transition-period values 2.5 GB / 20 GB / 50 GB). Definition: *"the amount of data that is read from or written to the run history of the cloud flow"*. Message size: *"Message size | 100 MB"*, *"Message size with chunking | 1 GB"*, with the caveat *"When you send files through a connector, the overall size of the payload and not just the file needs to be under 100 MB"* and *"some connectors and APIs might not support chunking or even the default limit"* (I-13).
- **Why it matters:** A document-moving integration is metered in bytes, not calls. On the Low profile, 200 MB/24 h is roughly two hundred 1 MB documents per day for the entire flow — an order of magnitude below what "500,000 requests/day" suggests. This is the meter that invalidates naive "move the invoices with a flow" designs.
- **Decision impact:** For any integration whose payload is files, size the design against the content-throughput meter and the gateway's 2 MB write cap (I-09), not the request meters. The documented alternative shape is to move the *bytes* out of the flow entirely — pass a reference (URL, blob path, valet key) and let the endpoints transfer directly. The corpus's supporting pattern is Valet Key (referenced in I-45).
- **Conditions:** Low-profile flows (Microsoft 365 seeded rights, per-app plans, free) are the ones most likely to be used for document work and have the tightest byte ceiling — the licensing-driven anti-pattern PS-43 again.
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-13, I-09; peer PS-43
#### IA-15 — Delivery guarantee, ordering, duplicate detection and dead-lettering are properties of the **transport**; picking the transport is picking the failure semantics
- **Classification:** DECISION CRITERION · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** I-12 (`ms.date` 2026-06-12) tabulates: **Delivery guarantee** — Event Grid *"At least once"*, Event Hubs *"At least once"*, Service Bus *"At least once (optional ordered, exactly once with sessions)"*. **Ordering** — *"No guarantee"* / *"Per partition"* / *"FIFO (sessions)"*. **Transactions** — No / No / **Yes**. **Duplicate detection** — No / No / **Yes**. **Dead-lettering** — Yes / **No** / Yes. **Capture / replay** — No / **Yes (Event Hubs Capture)** / No. Selection guidance: Event Grid *"React to status changes, serverless architectures"*; Event Hubs *"Telemetry, distributed data streaming, real-time analytics"*; Service Bus *"Order processing, financial transactions, workflows"*. And the events-versus-messages distinction: an event is *"A lightweight notification of a condition or state change. The publisher has no expectation about how the event is handled"*; a message is *"Raw data produced by a service to be consumed or stored elsewhere… A contract exists between publisher and consumer"*.
- **Why it matters:** It gives a defensible, non-product-first way to pick a broker: classify the payload as *event* or *message*, then read the required guarantees off the table. It also delivers the single most common correction — a business process that needs ordering and no duplicates cannot use Event Grid, however "event-driven" the requirement sounds.
- **Decision impact:** Requirement statement must include: at-most/at-least/effectively-once; ordering required yes/no and at what scope; duplicate tolerance; poison-message destination; replay requirement. Those five answers select the transport. If the answer to any is "we don't know", the design cannot be fixed and it becomes a decision-blocking Unknown.
- **Conditions:** *"In some cases, you use the services side by side to fulfill distinct roles"* (I-12) — one solution may legitimately use two or three. AT2-28 already records the same selection rule (command → Service Bus, discrete event → Event Grid, stream → Event Hubs); the addition here is the property table that justifies it. Quota envelopes for Event Grid and Event Hubs were **not fetched in this pass** (U-03).
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-12; peer AT2-28

#### IA-16 — A consistently throttled flow is **turned off** after 14 days; throttling is therefore an availability risk, not only a performance one
- **Classification:** RISK · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"Consistently throttled flows | 14 days | A cloud flow that is consistently throttled is turned off. Assign Power Automate Process licenses to the flow to dedicate capacity and avoid throttling."* Also *"Flows with errors | 14 days | A cloud flow that has a trigger or actions that fail continuously is turned off"* and *"Flows without trigger activity | 90 days"* (I-13). Restated in the guidance: *"Flows that violate them can be throttled, or slowed down, or even turned off altogether if they're throttled continuously for 14 days. Flows can be turned on again at any time, but if they continue to violate the limits, they continue to get turned off"* (I-22). And the sliding-window subtlety: *"if a cloud flow exceeds one of the limits, flow activity slows. It automatically resumes when the sliding window has activity below the limit"*, but *"Because these limits are for a single version, if you update your flow, it resets the limits"* (I-13).
- **Why it matters:** A design that runs permanently near a ceiling does not degrade gracefully; it is switched off, and the notification goes to the owner's mailbox. For an integration that another system depends on, that is an outage with a 14-day fuse and no external alarm. The "editing the flow resets the limits" behaviour is worse: it hides the problem from anyone who touches the flow.
- **Decision impact:** Any business-critical integration must (a) be sized with headroom against the smallest of the three meters, (b) have throttle-rate monitoring with alerting that is not the owner's inbox, and (c) have dedicated capacity (Process or per-flow licence) rather than relying on a personal licence. Microsoft's own remedy sentence names the licence as the fix — which makes licensing part of the reliability design.
- **Cross-reference:** AT2-01 (owner-licence dependency and the leaver scenario) is the companion risk; together they mean an integration's throughput and its continued existence both depend on an HR fact.
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-13, I-22; peer AT2-01

#### IA-17 — The **default retry policy differs by licence profile**: Low gets 2 retries, Medium/High get 12 — so identical flow definitions have different resilience
- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT + RISK · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"Low | This policy sends up to two retries at exponentially increasing intervals, which scale by 5 minutes up to an interval of approximately 10 minutes for the last retry."* *"Medium, High | This policy sends up to 12 retries at exponentially increasing intervals, which scale by seven (7) seconds up to an interval of approximately 1 hour for the last retry."* Configurable bounds: *"Retry attempts | 90"*, *"Retry maximum delay | One (1) day"*, *"Retry minimum delay | Five (5) seconds"* (I-13).
- **Why it matters:** This has not been recorded elsewhere in the corpus and it is a genuine surprise. Resilience is not a property of the design; it is a property of the *owner's licence*. The same flow, exported and imported under a differently-licensed owner, retries a transient failure twice instead of twelve times, and over a ten-minute window instead of an hour. A transient backend outage of 20 minutes is survived on Medium/High and lost on Low.
- **Decision impact:** For an integration with a stated reliability requirement, the retry policy must be set **explicitly per action** rather than inherited, and the licence profile of the owning identity must be recorded as part of the design. "It retried and gave up" incidents in Low-profile flows are a licensing symptom, not a code defect.
- **Conditions:** Retries consume request entitlement (*"Retries and requests from pagination also count as action executions"*, I-13) — so a longer retry policy trades reliability against the very meter that caused the failure. That circularity is real and must be sized, not ignored.
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-13

#### IA-18 — Concurrency knobs and the burst cap are the only back-pressure controls inside Power Automate, and they are coarse
- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"Concurrent runs | Unlimited for flows with Concurrency Control turned off; 1 to 100 when Concurrency Control is turned on (defaults to 25)"*; *"Apply to each concurrency | 1 is the default limit. You can change the default to a value between 1 and 50 inclusively"*; *"Concurrent outbound calls | 500 for Low; 2,500 for all others"*; *"Concurrent inbound calls | ~1,000"*; *"Read calls per 5 minutes | 6,000 for Low; 60,000 for all others"*; *"Invoke calls per 5 minutes | 4,500 for Low; 45,000 for all others"* (I-13). Burst cap: *"Action burst limits refer to the maximum number of actions that can be triggered in a specific period, typically measured in a rolling window of time. Currently, the cap is 100,000 actions in five minutes"*, with the remedy *"distribute the load between multiple flows using child flows or add trigger conditions"* (I-22).
- **Why it matters:** These are the whole toolkit. There is no rate-limit policy, no token bucket, no per-target quota and no adaptive throttle inside a flow. Where the requirement is "never exceed N calls/second to the target", the platform cannot express it directly — the design must either buffer (queue + worker with bounded concurrency) or place the limit in an API layer (I-20).
- **Decision impact:** Requirement "protect the target from us" → API layer or broker. Requirement "protect us from the source" → concurrency control (with the defect in IA-22) or a queue. Requirement "smooth a spike" → queue-based load levelling (I-44), because concurrency control queues only a bounded number of runs.
- **Absence claim:** No token-bucket / adaptive rate-limiting construct appears in the flow limits or coding-guidelines pages. **Treat as unsupported until verified** (INF; U-16).
- **Confidence:** HIGH on the quoted figures; MEDIUM on the absence claim · **Sources:** I-13, I-22, I-44

### 5.4 Transactions, consistency and the atomicity boundary

#### IA-19 — Atomicity stops at the Dataverse boundary, and the corpus has now confirmed this from three independent directions
- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** Web API: *"When multiple operations are contained in a change set, all the operations are considered atomic. An atomic operation means that if any one of the operations fails, the batch request rolls back any completed operations"* — but change sets are Dataverse `$batch` constructs, and *"GET requests aren't allowed within change sets"* (I-19). Connector: the Dataverse connector exposes *"Perform a changeset request | This action allows you to perform a group of Microsoft Dataverse connector operations as a single transaction. If one of the operations fails, all the successful actions are rolled back"* (I-33) — note "Microsoft Dataverse connector operations", i.e. same-connector only, which matches AT2-19. Peers: PS-45 and DA-07 already establish that canvas `Patch`, cross-connector flows and elastic tables are not transactional.
- **Why it matters:** It is the hard architectural fact behind most integration correctness bugs. "Write to SAP and to Dataverse, both or neither" is not expressible.
- **Decision impact:** For any multi-system unit of work: choose (a) single-owner write (all writes land in one system, the other is updated asynchronously from it), (b) compensating transaction with an explicit reversal path and a reconciliation job, or (c) move the orchestration to a platform with saga support. Record which of the three was chosen and what the failure window looks like. The reference architecture's expression of (a) is I-26's ERP write-back: pricing is composed in Dataverse, locked, then written to ERP, and *"ERP doesn't reprice finalized quote lines"* — one authoritative writer per lifecycle phase.
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-19, I-33, I-26; peers PS-45, DA-07, AT2-19

#### IA-20 — `$batch` semantics have three decision-relevant properties: a 1,000-request cap, all-or-nothing change sets, and **abort-on-first-error unless a header is set**
- **Classification:** FACT · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"Batch requests can contain up to 1,000 individual requests and can't contain other batch requests."* *"When an error occurs for a request within a batch, the batch request returns the error for that request and doesn't process any more requests. If you add the `Prefer: odata.continue-on-error` request header, you can specify that the server processes more requests when errors occur. The batch request returns `200 OK`, and individual response errors are included in the batch response body."* Change-set references: `$1`, `$2` allow one operation to reference an entity created earlier in the same change set, and *"Referencing a Content-ID before it appears in the request body returns the error HTTP 400 Bad request"* (I-19). Batching also raises URL-length headroom: *"you include the URL for the request in the body of the message where a URL up to 64 KB (65,536 characters) is allowed"*.
- **Why it matters:** The default is fail-fast, which for a bulk integration means a single bad row stops the remaining 999 and the caller must work out where it stopped. Most integration code wants `continue-on-error` plus per-item error handling; most integration code does not set it.
- **Decision impact:** Bulk inbound integration design must specify: batch size, `continue-on-error` or not, and the per-item error-capture destination. Combine with AT2-05's counter-intuitive Microsoft guidance — *"Avoid large batches… start with a small batch size of 10 and increase concurrency"* and *"Larger sized batches increase the chance you encounter execution time limits rather than limits on the number of requests"* — so the 1,000 cap is an upper bound, not a target.
- **Conditions:** Batch payloads must use CRLF line endings or deserialization fails (I-19). A batch whose payload items carry a non-matching boundary identifier *"succeeds without executing any payload item"* — a silent no-op that returns 200.
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-19; peer AT2-05

#### IA-21 — Synchronous outbound notification from Dataverse is a documented dual-write hazard: the transaction rolls back but the outbound call cannot be recalled
- **Classification:** RISK + ANTI-PATTERN · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"When you register a webhook for a synchronous step, it sends the execution context data to the configured endpoint immediately. If an error occurs after the request is sent, the data operation rolls back but the request sent to the configured endpoint can't be recalled"* (I-06). Independently: Service Bus *"only allows for asynchronous steps"* and Microsoft's instruction for Azure-aware plug-ins is *"register the plug-in to run asynchronously for best system performance"* (I-05, I-06).
- **Why it matters:** A synchronous webhook can tell the outside world about a business event that then did not happen. Downstream systems will have created records for a transaction that rolled back, and there is no compensating signal. This is the classic dual-write inconsistency and Microsoft states it plainly.
- **Decision impact:** Outbound notification of business events should be **asynchronous** (post-commit) by default. Where synchronous notification is genuinely required (e.g. a validating call to an external authority), the external endpoint must be idempotent and the design must include a reconciliation path. The corpus-sanctioned alternative shape for post-commit notification is the transactional-outbox family (I-04 references *"Implement the Transactional Outbox pattern"*) or Dataverse business events on completed operations (I-24: *"business event notifications only occur when these operations complete successfully"*).
- **Cross-reference:** DA-50 already records this from the data side; the addition here is the *architectural remedy* and the business-events alternative.
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-06, I-05, I-24, I-04; peer DA-50

#### IA-22 — Turning **on** concurrency control can cause triggers to be dropped, and it cannot be undone without deleting the trigger
- **Classification:** RISK + ANTI-PATTERN · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"Concurrency Control is set in the flow's trigger settings and is off by default. Turning on Concurrency Control can't be undone without deleting and re-adding the trigger."* *"Waiting runs | Not applicable when Concurrency Control is off; 10 plus the degree of parallelism (1-100) when Concurrency Control is on. This limit describes the highest number of flow runs that can be queued when the flow is at its maximum number of concurrent runs. **Note**: Additional triggers that arrive while the waiting runs limit is met might be re-tried by the connector. However, the retry attempts might not succeed if the maximum waiting limit continues to be met for an extended period of time. **To ensure all triggers result in flow runs, leave the Concurrency Control setting off in the flow's trigger.**"* Also: *"Split on items | 5,000 for Low without trigger concurrency; 100,000 for all others without trigger concurrency; **100 with trigger concurrency**"* (I-13).
- **Why it matters:** Concurrency control is the intuitive back-pressure knob and the most commonly recommended fix for throttling (it is the fix recommended by T3 source I-41: *"Reduce concurrency from default 20-50 to 1-5"*). Microsoft's own limits page says that enabling it can **lose events** and that the only way to guarantee every trigger produces a run is to leave it off. It also silently reduces the de-batching limit by three orders of magnitude (100,000 → 100).
- **Decision impact:** Do **not** use trigger concurrency control as the throughput remedy for an integration with a no-loss requirement. The correct remedy for that requirement is a durable buffer (queue) whose depth is unbounded by comparison, or a redesign of the producer. Where concurrency control is used, the loss risk must be accepted explicitly and the trigger settings recorded as irreversible.
- **Conditions:** The wording is *"might be re-tried by the connector"* and *"might not succeed"* — so loss is possible, not certain, and depends on the connector's own retry behaviour, which is not published per connector in the fetched evidence (U-17).
- **Confidence:** HIGH on the quoted behaviour; MEDIUM on the practical loss rate · **Sources:** I-13, I-41

### 5.5 Direct integration — when it is right, and how it fails

#### IA-23 — Microsoft's default recommendation for integration is Power Automate, on total-cost-of-ownership grounds, and this must be read as the *starting* position rather than the answer
- **Classification:** RECOMMENDATION · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"When selecting an integration tool, start with Power Automate as the default option. It offers unmatched cost-effectiveness for both development and maintenance. Power Automate is the preferred integration tool for many scenarios because it: - Delivers rapid development with low-code connectors - Minimizes long-term maintenance costs - Supports a wide range of triggers and systems - Scales well for most business scenarios."* And the exit condition: *"Custom code, Azure Functions, Data Factory, or Service Bus might give you more control or better performance, but they add complexity and cost. Use these options only when Power Automate doesn't meet your business or technical needs."* Plus: *"Every integration decision must consider the total cost of ownership. Custom solutions might seem powerful but often require a bigger budget for development, licensing, and support. Justify higher costs with clear business value"* (I-02).
- **Why it matters:** It is the strongest Tier 1 statement against reflexive over-engineering, and it is the counterweight the pack needs so that "add an API layer / add a broker" is not the automatic answer. Equally, the phrase *"Scales well for most business scenarios"* is a marketing-adjacent adjective that the source policy forbids treating as evidence (§7 of the source policy), and it stands in direct tension with I-38's *"Small to medium scale workflows"* (C-04).
- **Decision impact:** The pack's default option for a new integration requirement is a cloud flow with the appropriate connector, and every escalation must be justified by a *named documented constraint* from §3 or a *stated requirement* from §2.1 — not by preference or convention.
- **Conditions:** **Do not encode the adjective.** Encode the escalation conditions (§4) and leave the scale claim out; C-04 registers the conflict.
- **Confidence:** HIGH that the recommendation is stated; LOW on the scale adjective · **Sources:** I-02, I-38

#### IA-24 — The hybrid shape is Microsoft's own answer to "the flow can't do this bit": connectors for connectivity, code for computation
- **Classification:** PATTERN + RECOMMENDATION · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** The worked example is a loan-qualification process where *"Following an initial evaluation, the banking service considered cloud flow unsuitable given the complexity of the calculations. However, in this case a hybrid approach is the answer: - Power Automate to handle data collection with built-in connectors - Complex calculations encapsulated in custom code running as an Azure Function, which can be independently scaled, or in a custom connector"*, with the strategy stated as *"Don't choose tools in isolation. Instead, combine their strengths… Use Power Automate for orchestration and connectivity; Use Azure Functions for compute-intensive tasks; Use custom connectors to extend functionality when needed"* (I-02). The same division appears in I-26 (*"relies on Azure services for long-running asynchronous execution beyond platform limits"*) and I-29 (Service Bus + Functions for scheduling and delivery).
- **Why it matters:** It replaces the binary "low-code or pro-code" question with a **responsibility split** that has a documented seam: connectivity and human-facing orchestration stay in Power Platform; computation, long-running work and delivery guarantees move out. That seam is the most reusable architectural idea in the fetched material.
- **Decision impact:** When a single step is the problem, do not move the whole integration. Split at the step: keep the orchestration and the connectors, and externalise the offending capability (compute, duration, guarantee, protocol). Cross-reference AP-05 for the full pattern treatment.
- **Conditions:** The hybrid split imports a second operating model — two deployment pipelines, two monitoring surfaces, two skill sets. AT2-53 records this cost explicitly and it must appear in the option's cost, not be waved through as "we'll add a Function".
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-02, I-26, I-29; peer AT2-53

#### IA-25 — Webhook versus Service Bus is a scale-and-semantics decision that Microsoft states directly, and the deciding factor is who absorbs the burst
- **Classification:** DECISION CRITERION · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** Verbatim: *"Azure Service Bus works for high scale processing, and provides a full queuing mechanism if Dataverse is pushing many events. - Webhooks can only scale to the point at which your hosted web service can handle the messages. - Webhooks enable synchronous and asynchronous steps. Azure Service Bus only allows for asynchronous steps. - Webhooks send POST requests with JSON payload and can be consumed by any programming language or web application hosted anywhere. - Both webhooks and Azure Service Bus can be invoked from a plug-in or custom workflow activity"* (I-06). Also *"By using the webhook model, you can secure your endpoint by using authentication header or query string parameter keys. This approach is simpler than the SAS authentication model that you might currently use for Azure Service Bus integration"* (I-06).
- **Why it matters:** It is a rare, explicit Microsoft trade-off statement. The webhook is simpler to secure and can be synchronous; the broker absorbs bursts and gives queuing. There is no configuration that gives both.
- **Decision impact:** Requirement "the receiver is a modest web service and event volume is bursty" → broker, not webhook, because the burst lands on the receiver otherwise. Requirement "simple, low volume, any hosting, and possibly synchronous validation" → webhook, accepting the 60 s timeout, 256 KB truncation, single retry on 502/503/504 only, and the rollback hazard in IA-21.
- **Conditions:** Payload thresholds differ between the two paths — **192 KB** for the Service Bus/Event Hubs context (I-05) versus **256 KB** for webhooks (I-06). Both strip `ParentContext`, `InputParameters`, `PreEntityImages`, `PostEntityImages` above the threshold; the Service Bus path *fails the message* if it is still over 192 KB after stripping, whereas the webhook path adds a header and proceeds. That difference is decision-relevant for large records.
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-05, I-06

#### IA-26 — The Dataverse → Service Bus contract type is an architectural choice with different failure semantics per type, and **One-way requires a live listener or the message is eventually lost**
- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT + RISK · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** Contracts: *"Queue"* (*"a listener doesn't need to actively listen for messages on the endpoint… Dataverse supports a persistent queue. Persistent queues have a long but finite message availability duration that you can specify in code"*), *"One-way"* (*"requires an active listener. If there's no active listener on an endpoint, the post to the Service Bus fails. Dataverse retries the post in exponentially larger and larger time spans until the asynchronous system job that is posting the request is eventually aborted and its status is set to Failed"*), *"Two-way"* (*"a string value can be returned from the listener"*), *"REST"*, *"Topic"* (*"one or more listeners can subscribe"*), *"Event Hubs"*. Error handling: *"If the Service Bus is down or a listener or endpoint isn't available, the current message being processed in Dataverse isn't posted to the bus… For an internal Dataverse error, message posts aren't attempted. For an external service bus or network error, the related system job is in a Wait state"* (I-05).
- **Why it matters:** Publishing "to Service Bus" is not one decision. A One-way or Two-way relay contract couples Dataverse's egress to the consumer's uptime and eventually **abandons** the message; a Queue or Topic contract decouples it. Choosing the relay contracts recreates the tight coupling the broker was supposed to remove.
- **Decision impact:** For decoupled event publishing, choose **Queue** or **Topic**. Use One-way/Two-way only when a live synchronous consumer is genuinely part of the design and its unavailability is an acceptable failure. Monitoring must include the Dataverse **System Jobs** view, since that is where these failures land (*"check the status of the related system job in the web application for more information about the error"*, I-05) — an operational surface separate from flow run history (IA-42).
- **Conditions:** Persistent queue availability duration is *"long but finite"* and *"you can specify in code"* — no default is stated in the fetched page (U-18).
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-05

### 5.6 On-premises and the network boundary

#### IA-27 — The gateway's payload caps are the binding constraint on on-premises integration, and they are far below the platform's message limits
- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"Logic Apps, Power Apps, and Power Automate support both read and write operations through the gateway: - The gateway has a 2-MB payload limit for write operations. - The gateway has a 2-MB request limit and an 8-MB compressed data response limit for read operations. - URL for the GET request has a 2048 character limit."* Plus *"When you use the gateway with Power BI in DirectQuery mode, there's a 16-MB uncompressed data response limit"* and *"There's a limitation of 1,000 data sources maximum on each enterprise or personal gateway cluster"* (I-09). Custom-connector view of the same boundary: *"Maximum request content-length that can be sent to a gateway | 3,182,218"* (I-08) — see C-06 for the discrepancy.
- **Why it matters:** A flow can carry 100 MB (1 GB chunked) but only 2 MB of it can cross the gateway on a write. Document-oriented on-premises integration therefore cannot go through the gateway at all in its naive form; the bytes must travel another way (direct upload to cloud storage, a reference-passing design, or an on-premises agent that pushes rather than being pulled).
- **Decision impact:** Requirement "move files to/from an on-premises system" → size against 2 MB first. If payloads exceed it, the topology must change: either the on-premises side initiates (IA-04) and uploads directly to cloud storage, or a self-hosted component (APIM self-hosted gateway, an on-premises worker) handles the transfer and Power Platform handles only metadata and orchestration.
- **Conditions:** The 1,000-data-source cluster cap is a governance ceiling for large estates; the credential cache means a credential change takes *"approximately 5 hours"* to take effect (I-09, DA-34) — an operational surprise during incident response.
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-09, I-08; peer DA-34

#### IA-28 — VNet support and the Dataverse event-publishing path are **mutually exclusive** today: *"Azure-aware plugins don't support VNet"*
- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT + RISK · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"Do Azure-aware plugins support VNet? No, Azure-aware plugins don't support VNet"* (I-10, `ms.date` 2026-07-28). Azure-aware plug-ins are precisely the mechanism by which Dataverse posts execution context to Service Bus / Event Hubs / webhooks (I-05, I-06). Also excluded from VNet: *"Dataverse low-code plugins that use connectors aren't supported until those connector types are updated to use subnet delegation"*; environment types Trial and Dataverse for Teams are *"No"*.
- **Why it matters:** This is a genuine architectural collision and it is not obvious. An enterprise with a "no Power Platform egress over the public internet" policy and an event-driven Dataverse integration requirement cannot satisfy both through the standard mechanism. The corpus has not recorded this before.
- **Decision impact:** If private egress is mandatory **and** outbound events are required, the options are: (a) publish events via a **connector** that is VNet-supported (Azure Queues is on the supported list, I-10) driven from a flow rather than from an Azure-aware plug-in — accepting flow-level rather than transaction-level eventing; (b) publish from a **Dataverse plug-in** (VNet-supported, GA) writing directly to a private-endpoint-protected broker; or (c) accept public egress for the event path with compensating controls. Record which was chosen and why.
- **Conditions:** This is a point-in-time capability statement on a page that is actively evolving; re-verify before encoding. Whether option (a) or (b) is materially supported for Service Bus specifically was **not** established in this pass (U-19).
- **Confidence:** HIGH on the exclusion; MEDIUM on the workarounds (INF) · **Sources:** I-10, I-05, I-06

#### IA-29 — VNet support is an **outbound** control with a substantial set of preconditions, and enabling it breaks public calls by design
- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT + TRADE-OFF · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"VNet support uses Azure subnet delegation to manage outbound traffic from Power Platform at runtime."* Preconditions: *"to enable VNet support for Power Platform environments, you must associate an Azure subscription with the Power Platform tenant"*; subnets must be delegated in **both** paired Azure regions (*"if your Power Platform environment is in Canada, you need to create, delegate, and configure virtual networks in CanadaCentral and CanadaEast"*); *"allocate 25 to 30 IPs for production environments and 6 to 10 IPs for nonproduction environments"*; *"You can't reuse the same subnet in multiple enterprise policies"*; the subnet IP range and the VNet DNS **cannot be changed** while the feature is in use; *"Power Platform requires the endpoint to present a TLS certificate with the complete chain. You can't add your custom root CA to the list of well-known CAs"*. Consequence: *"When you use VNet support in a Power Platform environment, all supported services, like Dataverse plugins and connectors, execute requests at runtime in your delegated subnet and are subject to your network policies. **The calls to publicly available resources start to break.**"* Remedy: *"Internet-bound access is available by default from plugins and connectors in a subnet-delegated environment. Attach an Azure NAT gateway to the delegated subnet so your organization can control and secure outbound access"* (I-10).
- **Why it matters:** VNet support is often presented as a security toggle. It is an infrastructure programme: an Azure subscription relationship, IP planning across paired regions, a per-policy dedicated subnet, immutable network settings, a certificate-chain requirement that many internal endpoints fail, and a behavioural change that breaks existing public calls unless egress is deliberately re-provided.
- **Decision impact:** A "private connectivity" requirement must be costed as a joint Power Platform + network engineering workstream with named owners, and must include an audit of every existing plug-in and connector URL: *"Before you enable the virtual environment support for a Power Platform environment, check the code of the plugins and the connectors. Update the URLs and connections to work with private connectivity"* (I-10). Custom connectors created before delegation *"need to be resaved"* (I-07). Note also that OAuth traffic does **not** traverse the VNet: *"OAuth and token requests do not transit the linked Virtual Network. Only requests to API endpoints do"* (I-07) — so the identity provider must remain publicly reachable.
- **Conditions:** Supported region pairs are enumerated (I-10) and some regions have a single entry (Brazil, UAE, Singapore, Sweden, Italy) — the failover story differs there and was not further investigated (U-20). GCC is **not** supported; only GCC High and DoD are.
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-10, I-07; peers DA-36, DA-56

#### IA-30 — The two private-connectivity mechanisms answer different questions and Microsoft states the split explicitly
- **Classification:** DECISION CRITERION · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"A virtual network data gateway is a managed gateway that you use to access Azure and Power Platform services from within your virtual network without having to set up an on-premises data gateway. For example, the gateway is optimized for ETL (extract, transform, load) workloads in Power BI and Power Platform dataflows. Azure VNet support for Power Platform uses an Azure subnet delegation for your Power Platform environment… Power Platform API workloads use VNet support because the requests are short-lived and optimized for a large number of requests."* And the boundary: *"VNet support for Power Platform is the only supported option for all the scenarios for outbound connectivity from Power Platform except Power BI and Power Platform dataflows"* (I-10).
- **Why it matters:** Three mechanisms exist (on-premises data gateway, VNet data gateway, VNet support) and they are not alternatives for the same problem. Choosing the wrong one produces an unsupported configuration rather than a slow one.
- **Decision impact:** On-premises source + apps/flows → on-premises data gateway. ETL/dataflows/Power BI over a private network → VNet data gateway. Everything else outbound and private → VNet support. Note the incompatibility: under Power Platform VNet support for SQL, *"On-premise data gateway is not supported"* (DA-36) — so a design cannot mix VNet support and gateway on the same connector path.
- **Conditions:** DA-56 already records the "only supported option" statement; the addition here is the workload-shape rationale (*"short-lived and optimized for a large number of requests"*), which is what makes the choice predictable rather than arbitrary.
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-10; peers DA-36, DA-56
### 5.7 Integration security — the topology-driving parts only

Per the brief, this subsection is deliberately narrow: security facts are included **only** where they change the integration topology. Full treatment belongs to Area 06.

#### IA-31 — Power Platform managed identity is GA for **Dataverse plug-ins only**; every connector-based integration still needs a stored credential or a delegated user
- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"Power Platform managed identity allows enterprises to securely connect with Azure resources that support Azure managed identity from Dataverse plug-ins without the need for managing the credentials."* Supported-services table: **Area** Dataverse, **Services** *"Dataverse plugins; Dependent, assembly plug-ins"*, **Availability** GA. No connector, flow or app row exists (I-36, `ms.date` 2025-09-18). For contrast, Logic Apps Standard lists *"Managed identity authentication"* as a first-class capability (I-38, MS-V).
- **Why it matters:** "Use managed identity, don't store secrets" is standard enterprise security guidance and it is **not available** for the connector-based integration path. The consequence is architectural: a "no stored credentials" requirement forces the integration into a Dataverse plug-in, or out of Power Platform.
- **Decision impact:** Requirement "no secrets in the integration" → (a) Dataverse plug-in with managed identity to the Azure resource, or (b) the integration moves to a host that supports managed identity (Function, Logic App Standard, container), with Power Platform calling it via a connector. Where connectors must be used, the mitigation is a service-principal connection (available on the Dataverse connector: *"Service Principal | Provide Microsoft Entra ID credentials using Client Id and Secret"*, I-33) or certificate auth (*"Client Certificate Auth"*, I-33), with the secret in Key Vault surfaced through environment variables (I-27) — not elimination of the secret.
- **Conditions:** Whether managed identity will extend to connectors or flows is **not stated** in the fetched page (U-10). The federated-identity-credential mechanism behind it (UAMI or app registration + FIC) is described but its operational burden was not researched.
- **Confidence:** HIGH on the scope; UNKNOWN on the roadmap · **Sources:** I-36, I-33, I-27, I-38

#### IA-32 — Custom connectors cannot use the client-credentials grant, which removes the standard service-to-service OAuth flow from the connector path
- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"Q: What authentication types are supported? A: You can use these supported authentication standards: OAuth 2.0 for specific services, including Microsoft Entra ID, Facebook, Dropbox, GitHub, and SalesForce; Generic OAuth 2.0; Basic authentication; API Key (Except on-premises data gateway)"*, followed by the note *"Client credentials grant type isn't supported"* (I-08).
- **Why it matters:** For an unattended, system-identity integration, `client_credentials` is the normal OAuth flow. Its absence means a custom connector's connection carries either a user's delegated token or a shared secret/API key — both of which are identities that belong to *someone* and expire, rotate, or leave. It is the mechanical reason behind AT2-48's connection-identity problem.
- **Decision impact:** Unattended integration through a custom connector must plan for credential ownership and rotation as an operational process, or move the unattended leg to a mechanism that supports a system identity (Dataverse application user for inbound, plug-in with managed identity for outbound, or an Azure-hosted worker). Record the identity, its owner and its rotation cadence.
- **Conditions:** API Key auth is additionally unavailable through the on-premises data gateway (I-08), so on-premises custom-connector designs are pushed to Basic or Windows auth via the gateway's stored credential (DA-34's *"Regardless of the user, the gateway uses the stored credential"*).
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-08; peers AT2-48, DA-34

#### IA-33 — Delegated versus service identity is an integration-design fork, and Microsoft separates it from app authentication explicitly
- **Classification:** DECISION CRITERION · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"Connecting and authenticating to a data source is done separately from authenticating to a Power Platform service"* (I-37). The delegated path: the HTTP-with-Entra-ID connector *"empowers the connector to interact with these services using delegated access on behalf of the user… this approach fully respects the user's privileges and does not permit access to data or actions beyond their authorized scope"* (I-35). The service path: Dataverse business events from external systems require *"a special Application User account that you create in the Dataverse environment"* and *"server-to-server (S2S) authentication"* (I-24). Least privilege is the stated principle: *"An identity must not be allowed to do more than it needs to do"* and *"Power Automate flows access systems using least privileged accounts or a service principal where possible"* (I-37, I-30).
- **Why it matters:** The choice determines *whose* authorization the backend enforces, and therefore whether backend row-level security is still in force. A service identity concentrates all traffic on one identity — which also concentrates the Dataverse service-protection limit on one identity (DA-10) — while a delegated identity preserves backend authorization but spreads throughput across users and makes auditing per user.
- **Decision impact:** Per stream, decide and record: delegated (user's authorization applies at the backend; throughput per user; audit per user) or service (uniform authorization; single throughput bucket; audit shows one actor). Where backend row-level authorization is a compliance requirement, delegated is mandatory and virtual tables are excluded (IA-34). Where high throughput is required, a service identity is needed **and** must be distributed across several principals (DA-10, AT2-04).
- **Conditions:** Only Dataverse supports continuous access evaluation today: *"Currently, with Power Platform, only Dataverse supports continuous access evaluation"* (I-37) — so revocation latency differs by component.
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-37, I-35, I-24, I-30; peers DA-10, DA-33, AT2-04

#### IA-34 — Virtual tables cannot enforce row-level authorization or source-side user validation — Microsoft states the trade-off as a pro/con pair
- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT + TRADE-OFF · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** From the SAP reference architecture: *"Another option is to work with SAP through virtual tables, accessing SAP data as if you were accessing a Dataverse table. **Pro**: In Dataverse, a security role on the table grants table-level permission. **Cons**: Row-level permissions and user-level validation in the source aren't possible"* (I-25). From the developer guidance: *"Only organization-owned tables are supported. The security filtering applied to user-owned tables isn't supported… Field-level security isn't supported. Auditing isn't supported. Search functionality isn't supported… Charts and dashboards aren't supported… Offline caching of values isn't supported… A virtual table can't represent an activity and don't support business process flows… Once created, a virtual table can't be changed to be a standard (nonvirtual) table"* and, importantly for performance, *"Selecting attributes in Retrieve and RetrieveMultiple queries won't be applied since all attributes are returned"* (I-16).
- **Why it matters:** Virtual tables are the most attractive integration mechanism on paper — external data, no replication, Dataverse-native experience — and they discard most of what makes Dataverse an enterprise data platform. The "all attributes are returned" behaviour also means `$select` does not reduce the payload, which is a hidden throughput cost on wide external tables.
- **Decision impact:** Virtual tables are appropriate for **read-oriented reference data with table-level authorization and no platform-data-feature requirements**. The moment the requirement includes row-level security, audit, search, offline, charts or business process flows, the answer is replication (AP-07) with all its synchronization risk — which is exactly the decision I-28 documents: *"The team rejected virtual tables because the financial team needed to enrich records with department-specific attributes governed by strict row-level security"* and *"Using virtual tables isn't feasible when the secondary system's tables already exist and require row-level security."*
- **Conditions:** The OData v4 provider supports full CRUD, and custom providers now support full CRUD via plug-ins (I-16) — so "read-only" is a design choice, not a platform limit. The reference architecture nonetheless recommends *"Restrict access to the virtual tables to read access only"* (I-32).
- **Cross-reference:** DA-45 and PS-56 already record the feature losses; the addition here is the authorization consequence stated as a decision fork, plus the `$select` behaviour.
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-25, I-16, I-28, I-32; peers DA-45, PS-56

#### IA-35 — Secrets belong in a vault surfaced through environment variables, and the reference architecture shows the full shape
- **Classification:** RECOMMENDATION + PATTERN · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"The back-end API is secured by Microsoft Entra ID… The connector uses OAuth to authorize the user's access to the back-end API. The connector gets the client ID and secret from an Azure Key Vault using Power Platform environment variables"* and *"Azure Key Vault: Stores the credentials required to connect to back-end APIs using OAuth. Similar to the back-end APIs, Power Platform resources access the Azure Key Vault through the virtual network"* (I-27). Independently: *"Store secrets in Key Vault and access them via an integration identity. Avoid embedding secrets in flows, apps, or source control"* and *"Apply environment-specific connection references and configuration to prevent cross-environment leakage"* (I-26). And the general principle: *"Treat these secrets as entities that can be dynamically pulled from a secret store. They shouldn't be hard-coded in your apps, flows, deployment pipelines, or in any other artifact"* with *"Be sure that you have the ability to revoke secrets"* (I-37).
- **Why it matters:** It converts a security platitude into a concrete, ALM-compatible mechanism: Key Vault + environment variables + connection references, differing per environment. It also makes the Key Vault connector part of the integration topology (and it is VNet-supported, I-10).
- **Decision impact:** Any integration with a secret must specify: vault, environment-variable binding, rotation cadence, revocation path, and which environments hold which secret. AT2-50's run-history secret-exposure risk is the counterpart hazard on the flow side.
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-27, I-26, I-37, I-10; peer AT2-50

### 5.8 Enterprise systems and the system of record

#### IA-36 — System of record must be assigned **per entity and per lifecycle phase**, and the reference architectures do exactly that
- **Classification:** DECISION CRITERION + PATTERN · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** I-26 separates responsibilities by phase: *"Engineering-time configuration and pricing composition occur in Dataverse (Estimation/Planning apps, plugins, orchestration). Financial-time authority and execution occur in ERP (quote lifecycle, award, sales order, project/WBS cost tracking, production orders, MRP, scheduling, and posting)."* The advisory boundary is explicit: the planning app *"supports capacity visibility and lead-time communication. It's not authoritative—production orders, MRP (material resource planning), and scheduling remain in the finance and operation apps."* Key requirement: *"Maintain ERP as the authoritative system of record for quote lifecycle, award, sales order creation, project/WBS financial governance, production orders, MRP, scheduling, and posting."* I-28 does the same across two Dataverse environments: *"one environment acts as the authoritative source and another receives data."* Peer DA-48 already establishes the field-level granularity (*"the Sales app owns the customer name and contact information, while the Finance app owns the credit limit and payment terms"*).
- **Why it matters:** "Where is the system of record?" is not answerable per system; it is answerable per entity, per field, and — this is the addition from I-26 — per **phase of the lifecycle**. Composing a price in Dataverse and then locking it and handing authority to ERP is a legitimate, documented shape that a single-owner model cannot express.
- **Decision impact:** For each entity in scope, record: who may create, who may change which fields, at which lifecycle phase authority transfers, and what happens to in-flight records at the transfer. The transfer point is where the integration lives, and locking is the mechanism that makes it safe: *"Locked recap outputs preserve traceability of the pricing basis used at the time of pricing composition… Subsequent trade agreement changes intentionally don't retroactively modify finalized recap outputs. If repricing is required, execute a formal requote cycle"* (I-26).
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-26, I-28; peer DA-48

#### IA-37 — Microsoft's own ERP reference architecture **excludes financial posting entities from bidirectional sync by design**, to avoid a shadow ERP
- **Classification:** ANTI-PATTERN (named remedy) + RECOMMENDATION · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"Govern integration scope across three patterns—virtual entities (read), dual-write (write), and an OData API—with explicit exclusion of financial posting entities from dual-write."* And: *"**Financial posting entities (general ledger, invoice journals, cost transactions, production postings, accounting distributions, and financial dimensions) are deliberately excluded from dual-write, avoiding any shadow-ERP behavior.**"* Also *"ERP pricing APIs (including Commerce Scale Unit pricing interrogation) aren't invoked during iterative estimation or recap"*, and under Performance Efficiency: *"Align synchronization to business needs and avoid unnecessary Dual-write scope"* (I-26, `ms.date` 2026-08-11).
- **Why it matters:** This is the most valuable single statement in the fetched evidence for the "ignoring system ownership" anti-pattern. Microsoft, describing its own products, **restricts** the scope of its own tightly-coupled bidirectional sync mechanism and names the failure mode it is avoiding. "Sync everything because we can" is contradicted at the source.
- **Decision impact:** Sync scope is a design decision with a default of **minimum**. Entities that constitute the other system's authoritative ledger should be read (virtual entities) or queried (API), not replicated bidirectionally. The pack should require a per-entity justification for every bidirectional entity, and should treat "financial postings", "inventory ledger" and equivalents as presumptively excluded.
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-26

#### IA-38 — Dual-write is Microsoft's own name for a **tightly coupled** integration, and it changes the Dataverse schema
- **Classification:** TRADE-OFF + CONSTRAINT · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"Dual-write provides tightly coupled, bidirectional integration between finance and operations apps and Dataverse. Any data change in finance and operations apps causes writes to Dataverse, and any data change in Dataverse causes writes to finance and operations apps."* Infrastructure features include *"Synchronous and bidirectional data flow between applications"* and *"Synchronization, together with play, pause, and catchup modes to support the system during online and offline/asynchronous modes"*. Schema impact: *"To create parity with finance and operations applications, dual-write makes some crucial changes in the Dataverse schema"* — new `company` and `party` concepts, unified activities and notes, an opt-in currency change to 10 decimal places, and *"Date effectivity is added to Dataverse. It supports past, present, and future data on the same table"* (I-15, `ms.date` 2026-01-15).
- **Why it matters:** Dual-write is frequently treated as a free "it just syncs" capability. Microsoft's own description names it *tightly coupled* and *synchronous*, and it mutates the receiving data model. Both facts are architecture, not configuration: adopting dual-write commits the Dataverse schema to ERP-shaped concepts and couples Dataverse write availability to the ERP's.
- **Decision impact:** Adopt dual-write when the requirement is genuinely a shared master with near-real-time bidirectional flow **and** the ERP-shaped schema is acceptable. Otherwise prefer read-only virtual entities plus a narrow, explicitly-scoped write path (I-26's model). Record the schema changes as part of the decision, because they are hard to reverse: *"If you understand the plan, you can avoid some design and development rework in the future"* (I-15).
- **Conditions:** The failure semantics of dual-write — what happens to the initiating transaction when the far side fails, and how "play/pause/catchup" behaves under sustained failure — were **not established** in this pass (U-14). This is a material gap for any dual-write decision and must be closed before the pack encodes a verdict.
- **Confidence:** HIGH on the coupling and schema statements; UNKNOWN on failure semantics · **Sources:** I-15, I-26

#### IA-39 — SAP integration is a three-way mechanism choice with different protocols, auth sets and network paths — and the choice is constrained by the transport, not by preference
- **Classification:** DECISION CRITERION + CONSTRAINT · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"Power Platform offers two connectors to fit varying needs… The SAP ERP connector uses SAP Remote Function Calls (RFCs) protocol… also uses Business APIs (BAPIs) and tables, including intermediate documents (IDOCs). The SAP ERP connector works with SAP ECC and S/4HANA environments and any SAP product that runs on the NetWeaver stack"*, supporting *"SAP authentication; Windows authentication; Microsoft Entra ID using Kerberos; Microsoft Entra ID using certificates"*. *"The SAP OData connector can consume data from all SAP products that support the OData Version 4.01 protocol. Using the OData connector with SAP ECC or earlier versions requires configuration of OData endpoints that aren't included in the ECC out-of-box setup"*, supporting *"Basic authentication; Anonymous authentication; Microsoft Entra ID using Azure API Management; Microsoft Entra ID using SuccessFactors"* (I-18). The decisive network constraint: *"Because the virtual network data gateway transmits data using HTTPS, this option only works with the SAP OData connector. The SAP ERP connector uses RPC and BAPI"* (I-25). Third mechanism: virtual tables, with the authorization limitation in IA-34. Fourth, explicitly last-resort: desktop flows *"on the rare occasions when the connectors don't meet your requirements or for a one-time screen scraping need"* (I-25).
- **Why it matters:** The protocol decides the network path. RFC/BAPI cannot traverse the VNet data gateway, so an SAP ERP connector design **requires** an on-premises data gateway with all its payload caps (IA-27) and operating model (IA-28/§2.1 row 10). An OData design can use HTTPS paths and can be fronted by APIM for Entra ID auth — which is also the documented way to get Entra ID onto the OData connector at all.
- **Decision impact:** SAP integration requirement → establish first whether the needed operations exist as OData services (S/4HANA yes for many; ECC requires endpoint development, i.e. an SAP-team workstream). If OData is available, the design can be HTTPS/APIM/VNet-shaped. If RFC/BAPI is required, the gateway estate is mandatory and becomes part of the cost. Note the performance argument for using the API layer at all: *"The SAP ERP connector runs on SAP's API layer, which typically has better system performance than running the same activity in the SAP GUI. As more users transition off the SAP GUI and to an external user interface like a canvas app, CPU consumption on the SAP servers drops significantly"* (I-18) — a rare capacity argument *in favour* of the integration.
- **Conditions:** Gateway sizing for SAP is stated: *"requires a Windows virtual machine (VM) with at least 8 GB of RAM… you should set up a cluster of Windows VMs for deployment to production to avoid single points of failure. Set up a separate gateway for each environment"* (I-18). Network ports: *"port 33XX for single application servers or port 39XX for load-balanced connections"* (I-18). SAP OData connector throttle limits were **not fetched** in this pass (U-08).
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-18, I-25

#### IA-40 — Legacy systems without APIs push the integration into RPA, and Microsoft frames this as an exception with a named fallback
- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT + TRADE-OFF · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"In the rare and special instances where connectors don't meet your requirements, consider using desktop flows"* and *"Use desktop flows on the rare occasions when the connectors don't meet your requirements or for a one-time screen scraping need"* (I-25). The legacy reference architecture nonetheless leads with an API: *"A Power Automate desktop unattended robotics process automation (RPA) flow accesses the legacy system through an API. It sends a notification if the API isn't available"* (I-30). Peer AT2-37/AT2-38 carry the RPA runtime envelope (24 h max run, 12 h max queue, 500 queue depth, per-bot licensing, UI fragility).
- **Why it matters:** RPA is a legitimate integration mechanism for systems with no programmable surface, and it is the mechanism with the worst reliability and maintenance profile in the platform. The evidence positions it as exceptional and note that even the RPA reference architecture reaches for an API where one exists.
- **Decision impact:** Before accepting RPA as the integration mechanism, establish that no API, database, file interface or message interface exists — and that the owning team will not build one. Where RPA is chosen, the design must include availability checks (I-30's *"sends a notification if the API isn't available"*), an explicit UI-change fragility risk, and the per-bot licence cost. Prefer API-based automation on Microsoft's own stability grounds (PS-21: *"API-based automation preferred over UI automation for stability"*).
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-25, I-30; peers PS-21, AT2-37, AT2-38

### 5.9 Data synchronization — mechanisms, failure modes and the risk threshold

#### IA-41 — The documented synchronization shape is **two mechanisms plus a reconciliation pass**, not one pipeline
- **Classification:** PATTERN + RECOMMENDATION · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** I-28 (`ms.date` 2026-04-30) describes: event-driven Power Automate for *"fast, record-specific updates"* via an HTTP POST to a published endpoint consumed by a subscriber flow; **plus** dataflows for *"bulk operations, such as initial data population and synchronization"* running *"on a fixed schedule (for example, nightly or after another dataflow runs successfully) or on demand"*; **plus** explicit reconciliation: *"Nightly dataflows in the secondary environment correct any missed or failed event-driven updates."* Idempotency is designed in: *"Upserts are performed by using an alternate key to avoid duplicates. This method updates existing data and inserts new records when no match exists."* And the residual manual step is admitted: *"Manual intervention might be required for data quality problems (for example, missing keys)."*
- **Why it matters:** This is the most complete synchronization design in the fetched Microsoft material and its structure is the lesson: the fast path is best-effort, the slow path is authoritative, and the slow path *repairs* the fast path. A synchronization design with only a fast path has no correctness guarantee; a design with only a slow path cannot meet latency requirements.
- **Decision impact:** Any synchronization requirement should be answered with three named components: event path (latency), bulk path (correctness and initial load), reconciliation cadence (convergence guarantee). Plus an idempotency key — in Dataverse, alternate keys, with DA-53's limits (≤ 10 per table, ≤ 16 columns / 900 bytes, not on secured columns or virtual tables, reserved-character restrictions).
- **Conditions:** Dataflow limitations shape the split and are not negotiable: *"a dataflow can't change row statuses or delete records that are removed (absent) in the primary Dataverse environment"* — so status changes and deletions must be handled by flows, which is why the reference design has a post-dataflow flow. Dataflows also *"can't assign service principals as owners"* and require the connection to be manually re-established after each deployment unless isolated in their own solution (I-28).
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-28; peers DA-49, DA-53

#### IA-42 — Synchronization becomes an architectural **risk** rather than a mechanism at four identifiable thresholds
- **Classification:** RISK + DECISION CRITERION · **Origin:** INF over MS evidence
- **Evidence and derivation:**
  1. **Fan-out beyond one-to-one.** *"This architecture is designed for a one-to-one relationship: a single master data management (MDM) environment linked to another single environment. **Scenarios where one master environment must synchronize with multiple other environments require a more scalable or distributed solution**"* (I-28, MS). Beyond 1:1, per-pair pipelines multiply combinatorially — the point-to-point explosion in §6 X-01.
  2. **Bidirectionality without a per-field owner.** DA-48's field-level ownership is a prerequisite; without it two-way sync has no conflict rule and the last writer wins by accident. Microsoft's own bidirectional mechanism (dual-write) is *"tightly coupled"* (I-15) precisely because it is bidirectional.
  3. **Volume against the three meters.** *"When designing event-driven flows, ensure that action volume and concurrency remain within Power Automate service limits. High-frequency CRUD activity can trigger throttling, especially in scenarios where flows execute tens of thousands of actions per day"* (I-28, MS).
  4. **Copy count exceeding the reason for copying.** Microsoft's named anti-pattern is unambiguous: *"Too much data synchronized with Dynamics 365 for reporting purposes that overloads the database. You should use a dedicated data warehouse"* (DA-51, MS).
- **Why it matters:** Synchronization is the mechanism most likely to be adopted for convenience and most likely to become the system's dominant failure source. Naming the thresholds turns "is this too much sync?" into four checkable questions.
- **Decision impact:** If any threshold is crossed, the answer is not a better sync pipeline; it is a topology change — a hub (one authoritative store publishing events, consumers subscribing), virtualization instead of replication, or a dedicated analytical copy path (DA-19/DA-20) instead of operational sync. Record which threshold triggered the change.
- **Confidence:** MEDIUM (the thresholds are this file's synthesis; each underlying statement is MS-sourced) · **Sources:** I-28, I-15; peers DA-48, DA-49, DA-51, DA-19, DA-20

#### IA-43 — Freshness in a virtualization design is bounded by the *upstream* refresh, not by the virtualization — Microsoft states this explicitly
- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT + RISK · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"Dataverse virtual tables retrieve the reference data you need. They query the data warehouse in real time. This process means the request uses the warehouse data available at creation time. **However, the actual freshness of that data depends on how frequently the upstream source systems load and refresh the data warehouse**"* (I-32, `ms.date` 2026-08-18).
- **Why it matters:** "Real time" in a virtualization design means "real-time read of a possibly stale store". Teams routinely present virtual tables as the answer to a freshness requirement when the freshness is actually determined three systems upstream. This is the same trap as caching (IA-07) with the staleness hidden behind a live-looking query.
- **Decision impact:** For any virtualization design, the freshness requirement must be traced to the *original* source's refresh cadence, and that cadence must be recorded as a dependency owned by another team. If the requirement is tighter than the upstream cadence, virtualization cannot satisfy it regardless of implementation.
- **Conditions:** The same page's performance guidance limits the blast radius: *"Only expose the required datasets using virtual tables to minimize query load"*, *"Optimize Power Apps screens and Dataverse tables to retrieve data on demand rather than loading full datasets"*, *"Use the data warehouse for heavy processing, and keep only lightweight references in Dataverse to ensure the architecture scales"* (I-32).
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-32

### 5.10 Error handling, resilience and observability

#### IA-44 — Microsoft's transient-fault guidance contains four explicit prohibitions, and cascading retry is the one integration designs violate most
- **Classification:** RECOMMENDATION + ANTI-PATTERN · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"In most cases, avoid implementations that include duplicated layers of retry code. Avoid designs that include cascading retry mechanisms or that implement retry at every stage of an operation that involves a hierarchy of requests… For example, say one component makes a request to another, which then accesses the target service. If you implement retry with a count of three on both calls, there are nine retry attempts in total against the service."* *"Never implement an endless retry mechanism. Doing so is likely to prevent the resource or service from recovering from overload situations and to cause throttling and refused connections to continue for a longer time."* *"Never perform an immediate retry more than once."* *"Avoid using a fixed retry interval when you access services and resources on Azure, especially when you have a high number of retry attempts. The best approach in this scenario is an exponential interval strategy."* Strategy selection: *"use an exponential interval strategy for background operations, and use immediate or fixed interval retry strategies for interactive operations"* (I-14, `ms.date` 2025-08-18).
- **Why it matters:** A multi-hop integration (app → flow → custom connector → APIM → backend) has four places to configure retry, each defaulting to something. The multiplicative effect turns a transient backend blip into a self-inflicted denial of service against a system that is already struggling — and each retry consumes request entitlement (IA-10).
- **Decision impact:** Retry must be configured at **one** layer per hop chain, deliberately, and the other layers disabled or reduced: *"Many services and resources implement a built-in retry mechanism. You should investigate how you can disable or modify these mechanisms if you need to implement retries at a higher level"* (I-14). Record where retry lives for each stream. Prefer the platform's own signal where it exists: *"some exceptions or error codes (like the HTTP code 503, Service Unavailable, with a Retry-After header in the response) might indicate how long the error might last"* — and Dataverse's own guidance is to obey `Retry-After` rather than compute a rate (AT2-04).
- **Conditions:** The guidance also requires idempotency before retry is safe: *"Consider whether retrying the same operation could cause inconsistencies in data… if an operation that inserts a record into Microsoft Dataverse is repeated, it might cause incorrect values in the table. Or, if you repeat an operation that sends a notification to the user, they might receive duplicate messages"* (I-14).
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-14; peers AT2-04, AT2-20, AT2-21

#### IA-45 — Throttling rate is a **design-quality signal**, and Microsoft says so — which makes it a monitoring requirement, not just an incident
- **Classification:** RECOMMENDATION · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"Log transient faults as warning entries rather than as error entries so that monitoring systems don't detect them as application errors that might trigger false alerts. Consider storing a value in your log entries that indicates whether retries are caused by throttling in the service or by other types of faults, like connection failures, so that you can differentiate them during analysis of the data. **An increase in the number of throttling errors is often an indicator of a design flaw in the application or the need to add premium capacity to the environment.**"* Plus: *"Consider implementing a telemetry and monitoring system that can raise alerts when the number and rate of failures, the average number of retries, or the overall times elapsed before operations succeed is increasing"* (I-14).
- **Why it matters:** It separates two responses that get conflated: a rising throttle rate means either the design is wrong or the capacity is wrong, and the log must distinguish throttling from other faults for anyone to tell which. Combined with the 14-day auto-off rule (IA-16), a rising throttle rate is also an early warning of an outage.
- **Decision impact:** An integration's monitoring design must separate throttling (429 with `Retry-After`) from other faults, track retry counts and end-to-end elapsed time, and alert on **trend**, not on individual failures. Where Application Insights is the destination, note AT2-42's caveats (managed environments, not lossless) and that flow integration is preview.
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-14; peer AT2-42

#### IA-46 — Power Automate's error-handling surface is retry policy, run-after configuration and scopes; there is **no** documented circuit breaker or dead-letter construct
- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT · **Origin:** MS (the surface) + INF (the absence)
- **Evidence:** The Power Platform facilitation section names exactly three mechanisms: *"Power Automate includes a feature to retry an action if it fails. Configure this on a per-action level… Power Automate also offers actions to return custom errors and data to the calling app or flow… Configure error and exception handling in cloud flows by using scopes and run-after settings"* (I-14). Dataverse background operations add a fixed retry: *"If an error occurs during the execution of a background operation, Dataverse employs a retry mechanism. The system retries the failed request up to three times, using an exponential backoff strategy"* (I-17). No circuit-breaker or dead-letter construct appears in I-13, I-14, I-22 or I-39.
- **Why it matters:** Two of the resilience patterns the brief asks about — circuit breaker and dead-letter handling — have no in-platform expression. Their canonical implementations are external: dead-lettering is a Service Bus/Event Grid feature (I-12, I-44) and circuit breaking is either application logic or an APIM backend feature (*"Harden your API Management policies by using per-request timeouts, conditional error handling, and circuit breakers"*, I-46).
- **Decision impact:** A requirement for poison-message quarantine or for failing fast on a sick dependency is an **escalation trigger** to a broker or an API layer, not something to be approximated with flow variables. Where the requirement is soft, the closest in-platform approximations are: a status table plus a scheduled repair pass (I-28's reconciliation, I-26's job-status tracking) for dead-lettering, and a "dependency health" flag checked by trigger conditions for circuit breaking — both are hand-built and must be recorded as such.
- **Absence claim:** No circuit-breaker or dead-letter feature is documented for cloud flows in the pages fetched in this pass. **Treat as unsupported until verified** (INF; U-11, U-12).
- **Confidence:** HIGH on the available surface; MEDIUM on the absence · **Sources:** I-14, I-17, I-12, I-44, I-46; peers AT2-20, AT2-21

#### IA-47 — Asynchronous integration needs a **status resource**, and Microsoft's Power Platform guidance says the same thing three times in three places
- **Classification:** PATTERN + RECOMMENDATION · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** Well-Architected: *"If you require a background task to communicate with the calling task to indicate progress or completion, you must implement a mechanism such as: - Write a status indicator value to storage that's accessible to the UI or the caller task… - Expose an API or endpoint from the background task that the UI or caller can access to obtain status information… - Configure the background task to respond with the status or the data that it processed back to the UI"* (I-21). Reference architecture: *"Azure Service Bus dispatches long-running clone jobs to an Azure Function for asynchronous processing, and the function writes the job status to Dataverse"* and *"Track job status in Dataverse for recoverability and support"* (I-26). Notification architecture: a `Notification` table *"tracking the channel (SMS or Email), scheduled delivery time, status (Scheduled, Sent, Failed, or Canceled), number of attempts, and the associated Service Bus sequence number"*, whose purpose is *"real-time tracking, reporting, and error handling for all notifications"* (I-29). Dataverse background operations offer the platform version: *"you receive notifications by: Including a callback URL with your request. Subscribing to the `OnBackgroundOperationComplete` event"* (I-17).
- **Why it matters:** The status resource is what makes an asynchronous integration operable — it is simultaneously the UX affordance, the retry ledger, the reconciliation input and the audit trail. Designs that fire-and-forget without it cannot answer "what happened to message X", which is the operational requirement in §2.1 row 12.
- **Decision impact:** Every asynchronous integration must define a status entity with at least: correlation id, state, attempt count, last error, timestamps, and (where a broker is used) the broker's message identifier for cancellation. I-29's `Notification` table is the concrete template. This is also the honest answer to "we need dead-lettering" inside Power Platform (IA-46).
- **Conditions:** Well-Architected also warns about the UX cost: *"background jobs might require the user to wait for a notification, refresh the page, or manually check the status of the task. These behaviors can increase the complexity of the user interaction and negatively affect the user experience"*, and recommends a status list page or push notification instead of polling (I-21).
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-21, I-26, I-29, I-17

#### IA-48 — Trigger conditions are the documented remedy for both loop prevention and wasted executions, and the anti-pattern is filtering inside the flow
- **Classification:** RECOMMENDATION + ANTI-PATTERN · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"Indicate the Change Type to monitor. Define the columns to respond to using the Select Columns parameter. Use the Filter Rows parameter to ensure only customer-facing status changes trigger the flow… **Avoid implementing this logic in the flow itself using an `If` action. Use trigger parameters to reduce unnecessary executions and improve performance**"* (I-02). Loop prevention: *"Avoid loops where an event triggers an action that retriggers the same event. Prevent multiple updates from causing rapid, repeated notifications"* (I-02), and *"Make sure to use trigger conditions to prevent endless loops if, as part of the workflow, you change the data source that starts the workflow… Use trigger conditions to start the workflow only when fields that are changed by the application are updated, but not any other fields"* (I-21). The Dataverse trigger exposes exactly these parameters (`message`, `entityname`, `scope`, `filteringattributes`, `filterexpression`, `runas`) (I-33).
- **Why it matters:** In a synchronization design, the self-triggering loop is the most common catastrophic failure: system A writes to B, B's change triggers a write back to A, and the pair saturates both meters within minutes. The remedy is at the trigger, not in the logic, because logic-based filtering still consumes an execution and a request per event.
- **Decision impact:** Every event-driven integration must specify its trigger filter (change type, columns, row filter) **and** its loop-prevention rule. For bidirectional sync, the loop-prevention rule needs to be written down explicitly — typically a per-field owner check (DA-48) or a provenance marker on the row.
- **Conditions:** The Dataverse trigger's `filteringattributes` semantics are worth noting: *"The flow triggers if any of the columns is present in the update payload regardless of if data is modified"* (I-33) — presence, not change. A caller that PATCHes the full record will re-trigger everything.
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-02, I-21, I-33; peer DA-48
### 5.11 The API layer — justification and over-use

#### IA-49 — Microsoft's own justification for an API layer in front of Power Platform is composition, reuse, caching and telemetry — not security alone
- **Classification:** RECOMMENDATION + PATTERN · **Origin:** MS
- **Evidence:** *"When dealing with complex algorithms or with many data sources shifting the logic from the canvas app to a RESTful API can be a good choice to help keep your formulas within your Power Apps canvas application simple while moving more complex functionality server-side."* *"This approach can also allow the Power Apps application to act as an integrator of multiple backend services providing a single view to the user of data and logic from all the sources. Using the REST API approach, you can also shift the complexity of interacting with multiple other systems to component implementing the REST API and simplify the implementation of the canvas application."* *"In general, as the complexity of the canvas app formulas increase, you should consider this type of architecture. For example, when multiple data sources are needed to build a single view, using an API layer can help to deliver a performant experience, because the data response can be shaped server-side and delivered to the client more efficiently. The use of this middle-tier layer means that you can add a server-side caching layer and implement richer telemetry for the app."* The cost is stated in the same breath: *"This architecture offers flexibility, but also means that more code-first developer work is needed to develop and maintain the RESTful service and data layer"* (I-31, `ms.date` 2025-04-17). APIM adds the governance half: abstraction, key/JWT/certificate verification, *"Enforces usage quotas and rate limits"*, transformation, caching, *"Emits logs, metrics, and traces"*, and a developer portal for discovery (I-20).
- **Why it matters:** It gives five *testable* justifications for an API layer, each traceable to a requirement: (1) two or more consumers, (2) multi-source composition per view, (3) contract stability/versioning, (4) rate limiting or caching to protect a backend, (5) end-to-end telemetry. Where none of the five applies, the layer is unjustified — and the same page states the cost of adding it.
- **Decision impact:** Require one of the five named justifications, in writing, before adding an API layer. Then choose *where*: APIM for policy, governance, discovery and rate limiting; a Function or service for domain logic and composition (I-46's *"place that functionality in a dedicated custom service behind the gateway"*); a custom connector as the surface Power Platform consumes, ideally generated from APIM (I-23).
- **Conditions:** The cost is not only development. The layer introduces a deployment coupling that must be managed: *"With the dependency between the application and the REST API you should ensure you have a tested strategy to mitigate a roll out of either that develops errors after one of the components is updated"* (I-31). And the custom connector's own contract-change cost applies on every API change (I-07).
- **Confidence:** HIGH · **Sources:** I-31, I-20, I-23, I-46, I-07

## 6. Anti-patterns

Each entry gives the anti-pattern, the documented evidence that it is one, and the corrective. Ids are `X-nn`.

| Id | Anti-pattern | Why it is one (evidence) | Corrective |
|---|---|---|---|
| **X-01** | **Point-to-point explosion** — one pipeline per system pair, grown organically | I-02 names the end state and does not flatter it: *"This pattern results in a service-oriented architecture—sometimes humorously called 'spaghetti architecture'—where systems are interconnected through well-defined, purpose-built flows."* I-28 states the scaling break directly: *"This architecture is designed for a one-to-one relationship… Scenarios where one master environment must synchronize with multiple other environments require a more scalable or distributed solution."* APIM's B2B framing puts it in cost terms: *"APIs eliminate the overhead inherent in point-to-point integration"* (I-20) | Above the 1:1 case, move to a hub: one authoritative publisher plus subscribers (broker), or a shared API surface. | **X-02** | **The monolithic integration flow** — one flow that handles every system and every case | *"Creating one large flow to handle all integrations isn't practical. | **X-03** | **Over-consolidated integration logic** — one flow serving several business processes because they share a read | *"First, however, consider whether the logic you're creating is too rigid. | **X-08** | **Synchronous outbound notification from a transaction** | *"the data operation rolls back but the request sent to the configured endpoint can't be recalled"* (I-06, MS) | Post-commit asynchronous notification; business events for completed operations (I-24); transactional outbox where exactly-once publication matters (I-04) |

| **X-13** | **Duplicated transformation and business rules** — the same mapping implemented in a flow, a plug-in and a report | I-01 makes transformation a capability question to be answered once per stream (*"Assess what each system can do before, during, and after data transfer"*); the anti-corruption-layer guidance is to place translation in **one** boundary layer and keep business rules out of it: *"it's important to focus the anti-corruption layer on translation logic. Avoid placing business rules or orchestration in the layer"* (I-47, MS). Dataverse is the only store with server-enforced rules independent of the client (DA-60) | One translation site per stream. | **X-14** | **Ignoring system ownership** — replicating another system's authoritative ledger | *"Financial posting entities … are deliberately excluded from dual-write, avoiding any shadow-ERP behavior"* (I-26, MS); *"Too much data synchronized with Dynamics 365 for reporting purposes that overloads the database. ## 7. Negative evidence register

Collected deliberately, per the brief and per the source policy's search-behaviour rule. Each entry is a documented limitation, failure condition or cost — not a recommendation.

**Mechanism ceilings**
1. HTTP with Microsoft Entra ID: **100 calls / 60 s per connection**; base64 body encoding; *"Requests that include binary content… are not supported and may result in corrupted or unreadable files. 2. SharePoint connector: **600 calls / 60 s per connection** (I-34) — the ceiling behind the most commonly reported 429 incident (I-41).
3. Dataverse connector: **6,000 calls / 300 s per connection** (I-33), on top of service protection per identity (DA-10).
4. Custom connector: **CONFLICTED** at 500 or 10,000 requests/min per connection (C-01); count capped at 1 on Microsoft 365 / Dynamics 365 plans and 50 on per-user plans (I-08, I-13); **OpenAPI 2.0 only**; definition file < 1 MB; no `client_credentials`; no API Key through the gateway (I-08); REST only outside Logic Apps (I-08).
5. Gateway: **2 MB write payload**, 2 MB request / 8 MB compressed response for reads, 2,048-character GET URL, 1,000 data sources per cluster, ~5 h credential-cache lag, only last six monthly releases supported (I-09).

**Platform behaviours that degrade or remove function**

24. Continuous access evaluation is supported by Dataverse only (I-37).

|---|---|---|---|
| **Duplicate processing** | Any at-least-once transport, any retried write | *"consumers can receive the same message more than once. Design consumer logic to be idempotent"* (I-44); *"if an operation that inserts a record into Microsoft Dataverse is repeated, it might cause incorrect values in the table"* (I-14) | Alternate keys as the idempotency key + upsert, per I-28's design; DA-53's limits apply (≤ 10 keys/table, ≤ 16 columns/900 bytes, not on secured columns or virtual tables) |
| **Lost updates / silent divergence** | Fast path only, no reconciliation | *"Nightly dataflows in the secondary environment correct any missed or failed event-driven updates"* (I-28) — i.e. the reference design assumes the fast path loses updates | A scheduled reconciliation pass is **mandatory**, not optional, for any event-driven sync. Cadence is a requirement input |
| **Conflicting writes** | Bidirectional sync without a per-field owner | DA-48's per-entity and per-field ownership; dual-write's tight coupling as the alternative (I-15) | Per-field ownership matrix, or single-writer-per-phase (I-26), or accept last-writer-wins and record the acceptance |
| **Status and deletion drift** | Dataflow-based sync | *"a dataflow can't change row statuses or delete records that are removed (absent) in the primary Dataverse environment"* (I-28) | A companion flow for statuses and deletes, sequenced after the dataflow (I-28's design) |
| **Ordering violations** | Parallel consumers, or a queue without sessions | *"Introducing a queue between a producer and consumer doesn't preserve the original submission order under all conditions"* (I-44); ordering is per-partition in Event Hubs and FIFO only with Service Bus sessions (I-12) | Sessions (Service Bus), or partition by the ordering key, or design consumers to be order-insensitive |
| **Unbounded backlog** | Producer rate > consumer rate | *"the queue continues to grow and latency increases. Monitor queue depth and scale consumers within safe limits, or shed work at the producer"* (I-44) | Queue-depth monitoring and alerting; consumer scaling bounded by the *downstream* limit, not by the queue |
| **Overload displacement** | Autoscaling the consumer into a rate-limited target | *"Autoscaling without bounding consumers' aggregate downstream rate only moves the overload to downstream dependencies"* (I-44) | Bound consumer concurrency to the target's documented limit (Dataverse: per identity, DA-10) |
| **Self-triggering loop** | Bidirectional sync, trigger not filtered | *"Avoid loops where an event triggers an action that retriggers the same event"* (I-02, I-21) | Trigger conditions + provenance marker; note the presence-not-change semantics of `filteringattributes` (I-33) |
| **Poison message blocking** | Any queue-based consumer | *"Rather than letting these messages cycle indefinitely and block the queue, route them to a dead-letter queue"* (I-44) | Broker dead-letter queue (Service Bus, Event Grid — **not** Event Hubs, I-12) + depth monitoring; no in-flow equivalent (U-11) |
| **Fan-out multiplication** | More than one target environment/system | *"Scenarios where one master environment must synchronize with multiple other environments require a more scalable or distributed solution"* (I-28) | Publish once to a broker/topic, subscribe N times; do not clone the pipeline |
| **Copy proliferation for reporting** | Sync driven by a reporting requirement | *"Too much data synchronized with Dynamics 365 for reporting purposes that overloads the database. You should use a dedicated data warehouse"* (DA-51) | Analytical copy paths (Fabric link, Synapse Link — DA-19/DA-20), not operational sync |
| **False freshness** | Virtualization over a batch-loaded store | *"the actual freshness of that data depends on how frequently the upstream source systems load and refresh the data warehouse"* (I-32) | Trace the freshness requirement to the original source cadence and record it as a cross-team dependency |
| **Schema capture** | Dual-write adoption | *"dual-write makes some crucial changes in the Dataverse schema"* — company, party, date effectivity, currency decimals (I-15) | Decide with the schema change in view; prefer scoped virtual-entity reads plus a narrow write path (I-26) |

- **Classification:** RISK. **Origin:** MS for every basis cell; INF for the register's organisation. **Confidence:** HIGH on the bases; MEDIUM on completeness. **Sources:** I-02, I-12, I-14, I-15, I-21, I-26, I-28, I-32, I-33, I-44; peers DA-10, DA-19, DA-20, DA-45…DA-54.

## 10. Conflicts (CONFLICTED)

**C-01 — Custom connector requests per minute per connection: 500 or 10,000?**
- **Source A** (I-13, *Limits of automated, scheduled, and instant flows*, `ms.date` 2026-07-17): *"Number of requests per minute for a custom connector | 500 requests per minute per connection"*.
- **Source B** (I-08, *Custom connector FAQ*, `ms.date` 2025-03-13): table row for **Power Automate and Power Apps** — *"Number of requests per minute for each connection created by a custom connector | 10000 requests for each connection created by the connector"*; the Logic Apps table on the same page says 500.
- **Why they may differ:** B may be stale (older `ms.date`), or B's Power Platform row may have been raised and A not updated, or A may be the enforced value and B aspirational. Peer PS-26 independently recorded 500 from a third page, which weights toward 500.
- **Which is more authoritative:** A is newer and is the platform limits page; two corpus sources now say 500. **Provisional reading: 500.**
- **Decision impact:** A 20× difference on the main extensibility mechanism. A design sized at 10,000/min that is actually capped at 500/min fails by a factor of twenty.
- **Status:** CONFLICTED. Do not encode either figure without verification. **Sources:** I-13, I-08; peer PS-26.

**C-02 — Custom connector count: 50 per user, or one? (RESOLVED as scope, not contradiction)**
- I-13 states *"50 per user"*; I-08 states *"Free plan: one; Office 365 and Dynamics 365 plans: one; Per user plan: 50"*. Reading: I-13 states the per-user-plan case; I-08 is licence-conditional and is the more complete statement. **Resolution: use I-08's tiered list.** Decision impact remains material — on seeded Microsoft 365 rights the count is **one** (IA-13). **Sources:** I-13, I-08.

**C-03 — Does Power Platform bypass SAP authorizations? The same Microsoft page says both.**
- **Source A** (I-25, workflow step 3, `ms.date` 2026-07-14): *"On-premises data gateway: Calls SAP and uses SAP. **Power Platform doesn't bypass authorizations in SAP.**"*
- **Source B** (I-25, Considerations → Security, same page, same date): *"Stringent security and governance for developers and end users are vital when building SAP-connected solutions with Power Platform. **Power Platform bypasses authorizations in SAP.** You can ensure safety at many levels, such as using Microsoft Entra ID, tenant access, app access, and SAP authentication."*
- **Why they may differ:** most plausibly an editing defect, with B intended as "does not bypass" (the surrounding sentences argue for layered safety, which only makes sense if SAP authorization is one of the layers). Alternatively B is scoped to a specific auth configuration — e.g. a gateway stored credential (DA-34: *"Regardless of the user, the gateway uses the stored credential to connect"*) means every Power Platform caller reaches SAP as one SAP user, which *effectively* bypasses per-user SAP authorization even though SAP's own checks still run for that one user.
- **Which is more authoritative:** neither; same page, same date. The mechanism evidence (DA-34's stored credential; I-18's Entra-ID-with-Kerberos and Entra-ID-with-certificates options for SSO) supports a **conditional** reading: per-user SAP authorization applies **only** where SSO is configured; with a stored gateway credential it does not.
- **Decision impact:** decisive for any SAP design with a compliance requirement on SAP-side authorization. It determines whether SSO (Kerberos or certificates) is mandatory.
- **Status:** CONFLICTED. Provisional architectural reading: **treat per-user SAP authorization as absent unless SSO is explicitly configured**, and verify with the SAP security team. **Sources:** I-25, I-18; peer DA-34.

**C-04 — Power Automate's scale positioning (carried forward from `automation-architecture.md` C-03).**
- **Source A** (I-02, Power Platform Architecture Center, `ms.date` 2025-12-11, origin MS): *"start with Power Automate as the default option"*; *"Scales well for most business scenarios"*.
- **Source B** (I-38, Azure Logic Apps documentation authored by the Logic Apps team, `ms.date` 2025-07-18, origin **MS-V**): *"Scalability | Large scale workflows with high throughput and low latency [Standard] … Small to medium scale workflows [Power Automate]"*; *"Performance … Suitable for lower-scale automation, limited by shared resources in Power Automate"*.

B's *capability* facts (managed identity, VNet integration, private endpoints, resource-level RBAC, Git integration, deployment slots, configurable retention) are usable; its scale adjectives are not.
- **Status:** CONFLICTED, with a stated handling rule. **Sources:** I-02, I-38; peer conflict AT2 C-03.

**C-05 — Gateway maximum payload: 2 MB or 3,182,218 bytes?**
- **Source A** (I-09, `ms.date` 2025-06-10): *"The gateway has a 2-MB payload limit for write operations. The gateway has a 2-MB request limit and an 8-MB compressed data response limit for read operations."*

## 12. When Power Platform should **not** be the integration layer

This is the section the brief marks critical, and the corpus's core rule applies: *"Power Platform must NOT be assumed to be the answer"* (`../README.md`). The default remains Microsoft's own — start with Power Automate (IA-23) — but the following conditions move the integration responsibility elsewhere. Crucially, **"elsewhere" is not automatically Azure**: where the enterprise already owns an integration capability, that capability is the answer regardless of vendor.

### 12.1 The integration responsibility already has an owner

**Condition.** An enterprise integration platform, iPaaS, ESB, API gateway or message backbone already exists, and the system in question is already integrated with it or is in scope for it.

**Why this comes first.** Nothing in the fetched Microsoft material tells an architect to check this, which is precisely why it must be step 1 of the classification test (§2.2). The consequences of skipping it are the anti-patterns X-01 (point-to-point explosion) and X-13 (duplicated transformation), plus a governance outcome no one chose: business-critical integrations owned by a maker, throttled by a personal licence (IA-16, AT2-01), monitored through a portal (X-18), and invisible to the team that owns integration.

**Architectural consequence.** Power Platform consumes a published contract — ideally as a custom connector generated from the platform's OpenAPI definition, or via APIM's export-to-Power-Platform path when the gateway is APIM (I-23). The integration logic, the transformation, the retry policy, the dead-lettering and the monitoring stay with their owner. Power Platform's remit shrinks to *experience and human workflow*, which is where its documented strengths are (I-11: *"Power Automate empowers business users, office workers, and citizen developers to build simple integrations"*).

- **Classification:** DECISION CRITERION · **Origin:** INF (the priority ordering) over MS mechanism evidence (I-23, I-20) · **Confidence:** MEDIUM

### 12.2 The requirement is B2B / EDI / partner protocol handling

**Condition.** Trading-partner agreements, EDI/X12/EDIFACT, AS2, schemas and maps, partner onboarding.

**Evidence.** Microsoft assigns this explicitly to Logic Apps, not Power Automate: Logic Apps Standard integration includes *"B2B capabilities"* and the *"Enterprise Integration Pack for B2B scenarios"*; Power Automate's row in the same table lists connectors only (I-38 **MS-V** for the positioning, I-11 for the Enterprise Integration Pack as a capability fact). I-11 also names B2B as a Logic Apps example: *"Examples include B2B processes or scenarios that require enterprise-level interactions with Azure DevOps."*

**Architectural consequence.** B2B protocol handling belongs to an integration platform with a trading-partner model. Power Platform participates downstream of it.

- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT · **Origin:** MS (capability) + MS-V (positioning) · **Confidence:** HIGH on the capability gap

### 12.3 Strict cross-system transactionality or saga compensation is required

**Condition.** A unit of work spans two or more systems and must succeed or fail as one, or requires a formal compensation/saga model with state.

**Evidence.** Atomicity stops at the Dataverse boundary (IA-19, PS-45, DA-07). Change sets are Dataverse-only (I-19, I-33). The synchronous-webhook path is a documented dual-write hazard (IA-21). Nothing in the fetched evidence provides cross-system transaction coordination or a saga construct in Power Platform; the transactional-outbox and compensating-transaction patterns are catalogued on the Azure side (I-04, I-45).

**Architectural consequence.** Either collapse the unit of work into one system's transaction (the I-26 shape: compose in one place, lock, hand authority over), or move the orchestration to a host with durable state and compensation (Durable Functions, Logic Apps Standard stateful, a bespoke service). `automation-architecture.md`'s PROCESS ORCHESTRATION shape is the same conclusion reached from the automation side.

- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT · **Origin:** MS · **Confidence:** HIGH

### 12.4 Sustained throughput exceeds the three-meter envelope with no natural partitioning

**Condition.** Required sustained rate exceeds the smallest of {entitlement, service protection per identity, connector throttle per connection}, and the load cannot be partitioned across connections, identities or flows.

**Evidence.** The meters and their independence (IA-10; AT2-02 *"Batch operations aren't a valid strategy to bypass entitlement limits. Service protection API limits and entitlement limits are evaluated separately"*). The auto-off rule makes sustained over-limit operation an availability failure, not a performance one (IA-16). Microsoft's own high-volume design moves the work out: *"overcoming the limitations of relying solely on Power Automate for orchestrating large volumes of notifications"*, with Service Bus for scheduling and Functions for delivery *"without the overhead that Power Automate-based delays would introduce"* (I-29).

**Architectural consequence.** Power Platform keeps the user-facing and business-record parts; a broker plus workers takes the volume. Note the partitioning caveat: distributing across identities is a documented mitigation (DA-10) but it multiplies identity management and audit surface, so it is a bounded remedy, not an escape.

- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT · **Origin:** MS · **Confidence:** HIGH

### 12.5 The integration must execute inside a network Power Platform cannot enter

**Condition.** The endpoint is unreachable by the gateway (protocol not supported, e.g. non-HTTP/non-supported-connector traffic), or the endpoint presents a TLS certificate not chained to a well-known root CA, or the environment type does not support VNet (Trial, Dataverse for Teams), or GCC, or both private egress and Dataverse event publishing are required simultaneously.

**Evidence.** *"Power Platform requires the endpoint to present a TLS certificate with the complete chain. You can't add your custom root CA to the list of well-known CAs"*; Trial and Teams *"No"*; GCC unsupported; *"Azure-aware plugins don't support VNet"* (I-10). Gateway payload caps of 2 MB (I-09). RFC/BAPI cannot use the VNet data gateway (I-25).

**Architectural consequence.** Place an integration component inside the network — an APIM self-hosted gateway (*"an API provider can deploy the API gateway to the same environments where they host their APIs, to optimize API traffic and ensure compliance with local regulations and guidelines… packaged as a Linux-based Docker container"*, I-20), an on-premises worker, or the enterprise's existing on-premises integration runtime — and let Power Platform reach that component over a supported path.

- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT · **Origin:** MS · **Confidence:** HIGH

### 12.6 Message-level audit, replay or long retention is required

**Condition.** The requirement includes proving what happened to an individual message, replaying a failed batch, or retaining integration history beyond what run history offers.

**Evidence.** Power Automate's monitoring row: *"Basic monitoring through the Power Automate portal - Custom injection into Application Insights"*; *"Version control | Limited versioning"* (I-38, **MS-V**, capability facts). Application Insights for flows is preview, managed-environments-only and *"not 100% lossless"* (AT2-42). No dead-letter or replay construct is documented (U-11). Tier 1 retention for run history was not established (U-21).

**Architectural consequence.** A durable message log outside the flow: a broker with dead-lettering plus a status table (the I-26 and I-29 shape), or the enterprise integration platform's own message store. Note that this is an *operational* requirement driving a topology change — exactly the §2.1 row 12 chain.

- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT · **Origin:** MS-V (capability) + MS (AT2-42) + UNKNOWN (retention) · **Confidence:** MEDIUM

### 12.7 The integration must be delivered and operated as code

**Condition.** Requirements include local development and debugging, infrastructure-as-code, branch-based review of the integration definition, zero-downtime deployment, or resource-level RBAC so that an author's departure does not affect access.

**Evidence.** Logic Apps Standard capability facts from I-38 (**MS-V**): local development in Visual Studio Code; *"Full Git integration using CI/CD pipelines"*; ARM/Bicep infrastructure-as-code; *"zero downtime deployment when you set up deployment slots"*; and the RBAC contrast stated plainly — *"In Azure Logic Apps, RBAC works at the resource level… So, if the workflow creator leaves, you don't lose access to their workflows. … In Power Automate, RBAC works at the user level."* Power Platform's counter-evidence is real and must be weighed: it has a documented production ALM stack (solutions, pipelines, native Git integration — PS-48).
**Handling note.** Use the capability facts; do **not** carry I-38's scale adjectives (C-04).

**Architectural consequence.** Where the integration is genuinely a software asset with a release process of its own, a code-first host is the better home. Where the integration is a business process with a solution-based lifecycle, Power Platform's ALM stack is adequate (PS-48). The discriminator is whether the *integration definition* needs the software lifecycle, not whether the organisation likes DevOps.

- **Classification:** TRADE-OFF · **Origin:** MS-V (capability) + MS (PS-48) · **Confidence:** MEDIUM

### 12.8 The integration is bulk data movement or transformation at scale

**Condition.** ETL/ELT, large transformations, warehouse loading, analytical copies.

**Evidence.** Microsoft's own statement that complex business logic and large-scale transformation do not belong in cloud flows (PS-07); the named anti-pattern of syncing to Dataverse for reporting instead of using a warehouse (DA-51); the alternatives are Data Factory (I-02 names it as an escalation target; I-04 lists it as an Azure integration service *"Visually integrate data sources to accelerate data transformation"*) and the analytical copy paths (DA-19 Fabric link, DA-20 Synapse Link). Caching guidance itself points at Synapse Link (I-01).

**Architectural consequence.** A data pipeline tool owns bulk movement; Power Platform consumes the result. This is a boundary the pack should enforce rather than negotiate.

- **Classification:** CONSTRAINT · **Origin:** MS · **Confidence:** HIGH

### 12.9 What is *not* a reason to move the integration out

Recorded to keep the boundary honest:
- **Scale anxiety without a number.** The three meters are published; compute them. *"Scales effortlessly"* and *"small to medium scale"* are both marketing per the source policy (§7).
- **A preference for code.** I-02's total-cost-of-ownership instruction cuts the other way: *"Custom solutions might seem powerful but often require a bigger budget for development, licensing, and support. Justify higher costs with clear business value."*
- **One difficult step.** The documented answer is the hybrid split at the step (IA-24), not relocating the whole integration.
- **Resemblance to a reference architecture.** The brief's FINAL TEST is explicit on this point.
